What the Lab Achieves

A model is only as trustworthy as the evidence that supports it. Throughout this text we have built the machinery of probability distributions, parameter estimation, and hypothesis testing. The goodness_of_fit_lab draws every thread together into a single, executable question: does the distribution we assumed actually describe the data we collected?

Formally, let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent observations drawn from an unknown distribution $F$. We propose a null hypothesis $H_0 : F = F_0$, where $F_0$ is a fully specified or parametrically estimated distribution. The lab operationalises this test through Pearson's $\chi^2$ statistic,

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{(O_k - E_k)^2}{E_k},$$

where $O_k$ is the observed count in bin $k$ and $E_k = n \cdot p_k$ is the expected count under $F_0$. Under $H_0$, this statistic converges in distribution to $\chi^2_{K - 1 - r}$, where $r$ is the number of parameters estimated from the data. A $p$-value below a chosen significance level $\alpha$ is evidence against $F_0$.

The lab is the natural endpoint because it closes the modelling loop: propose, fit, test, decide. Every earlier concept — random variables, density functions, maximum-likelihood estimation, degrees of freedom — is a prerequisite, and none of them is complete without the verdict that goodness-of-fit delivers.

A Worked Example

Suppose we roll a six-sided die $n = 120$ times and record the face frequencies $O = (18, 22, 17, 21, 20, 22)$. Under a fair-die null hypothesis, $E_k = 20$ for all $k$. Then

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(18-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20} + \cdots = \frac{4+4+9+1+0+4}{20} = 1.10.$$

With $K - 1 = 5$ degrees of freedom, the $p$-value is approximately $0.95$, far above any standard $\alpha$. We do not reject fairness — the die is consistent with the uniform distribution.

Fundamental Theorem

Theorem (Pearson, 1900). Let $O_1, \ldots, O_K$ be multinomial counts with cell probabilities $p_1, \ldots, p_K$ summing to one, and let all $E_k = np_k \to \infty$. Then $\chi^2 \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{K-1}$. If $r$ parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood from the grouped data, the limiting degrees of freedom reduce to $K - 1 - r$.

This result is remarkable: a single asymptotic family, the chi-squared, governs the fit of any discrete or discretised continuous model, regardless of its specific shape.

Lab Cell

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

# Observed die rolls
observed = np.array([18, 22, 17, 21, 20, 22])
expected = np.full(6, 20.0)          # fair-die expectation

chi2_stat, p_value = stats.chisquare(f_obs=observed, f_exp=expected)
print(f"χ² = {chi2_stat:.4f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")

# Symbolic verification of the statistic
O, E = sp.symbols('O E', positive=True)
term = (O - E)**2 / E
residuals = [(18,20),(22,20),(17,20),(21,20),(20,20),(22,20)]
chi2_sym = sum(term.subs({O: o, E: e}) for o, e in residuals)
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(chi2_sym))

Run this cell and confirm that the symbolic sum matches chi2_stat. Vary the observed counts, re-run, and observe how the $p$-value responds — this tactile experiment is the fastest route to genuine intuition about the test's sensitivity.

An Invitation

The methods you have practised here extend far beyond dice. The same chi-squared framework diagnoses whether survival times in a clinical trial follow an exponential model, whether word frequencies in a corpus obey Zipf's law, and whether daily returns in a financial series are consistent with normality. Each of these domains uses goodness-of-fit not as a final verdict but as a navigational instrument: a failed test tells you where the model is wrong, which is precisely where the interesting science begins.
