Payoff

Throughout this text, we have built the machinery of linear algebra and probability piece by piece—vector spaces, bases, dimension, probability measures, and conditional expectation. The concept of independence stands at the intersection of all these threads, and the independence laboratory is where that intersection becomes operational. It is not merely a final exercise; it is the point at which every prior definition earns its payoff.

What the lab achieves. Formal independence—whether of vectors, events, or random variables—is the property that knowledge of one object carries no information about another. For vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, linear independence means the only solution to $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ is $c_i = 0$ for all $i$. For events $A$ and $B$ on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, independence means $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. For random variables $X$ and $Y$, independence means their joint density (or measure) factors: $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$. The lab brings all three definitions into a single computational environment so the student can verify, falsify, and build intuition simultaneously.

Worked example. Consider $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0, 2)^\top$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1, -1)^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Forming the matrix $M = [\mathbf{v}_1 \mid \mathbf{v}_2]$ and row-reducing yields two pivots; rank$(M) = 2 = k$, confirming linear independence. Now let $X \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $Y = X^2$. Although $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[X^3] - E[X]E[X^2] = \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{12} \neq 0$, we can verify directly that $f_{X,Y}$ does not factor, so $X$ and $Y$ are dependent—a canonical reminder that zero covariance does not imply independence.

Key theorem (Factorization Criterion). Random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are mutually independent if and only if their joint moment-generating function factors: $M_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(t_1,\ldots,t_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t_i)$ for all $(t_1,\ldots,t_n)$ in a neighborhood of the origin. This theorem bridges the algebraic factorization of densities with the analytic factorization of transforms, unifying the probabilistic and algebraic notions of independence under one roof.

Lab cell (SymPy).

from sympy import symbols, integrate, exp, Rational, simplify

x, y, t1, t2 = symbols('x y t1 t2', real=True)

# Joint density of two independent Exp(1) variables
f_joint = exp(-x) * exp(-y)

# Verify marginals recover the joint
f_X = integrate(f_joint, (y, 0, float('inf')))   # exp(-x)
f_Y = integrate(f_joint, (x, 0, float('inf')))   # exp(-y)

print(simplify(f_joint - f_X * f_Y))  # should print 0

The zero residual is not a numerical accident—it is the algebraic signature of independence, made visible.

Invitation. The independence lab unlocks a broad frontier. A student comfortable with the factorization criterion is ready to ask: when do the features of a dataset behave as independent signals, and when do hidden correlations betray a latent common cause? That question is the entry point to principal component analysis, Bayesian networks, and causal inference. Choose one of those paths, carry the definition of independence with you, and you will find it waiting at every decisive step.
