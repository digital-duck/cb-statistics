Concept Book: Independence Lab

Payoff

Throughout this text, we have built a ladder of ideas: sets, relations, functions, vector spaces, and probability measures. Each rung prepared us for the one above. The concept of independence — whether of events, random variables, or vectors — is the rung where these threads converge into a single, powerful principle: knowing one thing tells you nothing new about another. The independence_lab is the natural endpoint of this journey because independence is not merely a property to be verified; it is a lens through which every preceding concept becomes sharper and more useful.

What independence_lab achieves. At its core, the lab trains you to move between three equivalent characterizations of independence and to apply each where it is most computationally convenient. For events $A$ and $B$ on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, independence means

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).$$

For random variables $X$ and $Y$, it means the joint density (or mass function) factors:

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y).$$

For vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, linear independence means no non-trivial linear combination vanishes:

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0} \implies c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0.$$

Recognizing when these definitions agree — and when they diverge — is the analytical skill the lab develops.

A worked example. Suppose $X \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $Y = X^2$. Then $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = E[X^3] - E[X]E[X^2] = \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{12} \neq 0$, so $X$ and $Y$ are not even uncorrelated, let alone independent. Now let $U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $V \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ be drawn from separate, unlinked experiments. Their joint density is $f_{U,V}(u,v) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$ on the unit square, factoring cleanly — independence confirmed by inspection.

Key theorem (Factorization criterion). $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if there exist functions $g$ and $h$ such that $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = g(x)\,h(y)$ for all $(x,y)$. This criterion reduces a joint statement to a purely algebraic one, making independence testable with SymPy in a single cell.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, exp, integrate, factor, simplify

x, y = symbols('x y', real=True)
f_joint = exp(-x - y)          # candidate joint density on x,y >= 0
f_x = integrate(f_joint, (y, 0, oo))
f_y = integrate(f_joint, (x, 0, oo))
print(simplify(f_joint - f_x * f_y))   # 0 confirms independence

Independence is the natural endpoint because every application domain in this text — from Bayesian inference (prior and likelihood decouple) to linear regression (regressors must be linearly independent for the normal equations to have a unique solution) to signal processing (independent noise components add in power, not amplitude) — requires you to decide, rigorously, whether a factorization holds. Master this one test, and you hold the key to all of them.

Choose one domain that speaks to your own curiosity and carry the independence criterion all the way through its central theorem. The tools are now in your hands.
