Concept Book: Randomized Experiment

Introductory Statistics Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A randomized experiment is the logical endpoint of the inferential journey that begins with observation and advances through statistical modeling. Every earlier technique in this text — descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, regression, and analysis of variance — answers the question what is associated with what. The randomized experiment answers a fundamentally harder question: what causes what. That distinction is not merely rhetorical. Without randomization, any observed difference between groups can be attributed to pre-existing differences in the groups themselves, a threat known as confounding. Randomization — the act of assigning units to treatment and control conditions by a probability mechanism transparent to the investigator — severs the causal link between potential confounders and treatment assignment, so that the only systematic difference remaining between groups is the treatment itself.

Formal statement. Let $Y_i(1)$ and $Y_i(0)$ denote the potential outcomes for unit $i$ under treatment and control, respectively. The average treatment effect is

$$\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)].$$

Because $\tau$ requires observing each unit in both states simultaneously, it is inherently unidentifiable from a single observation. Under simple random assignment with $n_1$ units treated and $n_0$ units in control, the difference-in-means estimator

$$\hat{\tau} = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0$$

is unbiased for $\tau$, a result that follows directly from the fact that $\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}_1] = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)]$ when assignment is independent of potential outcomes.

Worked example. A study enrolls 200 students and randomly assigns 100 to receive a supplementary tutorial module. Post-test scores yield $\bar{Y}_1 = 74.3$ and $\bar{Y}_0 = 69.8$, giving $\hat{\tau} = 4.5$ points. Because assignment was random, this estimate carries a causal interpretation: the module caused the improvement, not a pre-existing aptitude gap.

Lab cell (SymPy).

from sympy import symbols, Eq, solve, Rational

tau, Y1_bar, Y0_bar = symbols('tau Y1_bar Y0_bar')
eq = Eq(tau, Y1_bar - Y0_bar)
print(solve(eq.subs({Y1_bar: Rational(743,10), Y0_bar: Rational(698,10)}), tau))

Connection to research ethics and IRB oversight. The same randomization machinery that confers causal power also creates an ethical burden. When a researcher withholds a potentially beneficial treatment from the control group, or exposes participants to an uncertain risk, a calculus of harm and benefit must be conducted before a single subject is enrolled. This is precisely the jurisdiction of an Institutional Review Board (IRB). The IRB evaluates whether equipoise — genuine uncertainty about which arm is superior — justifies the experiment, whether informed consent procedures are adequate, and whether vulnerable populations are protected from coercion. In this sense, randomized experiment and IRB oversight are inseparable: the design that maximizes causal validity simultaneously maximizes the investigator's moral exposure, and the IRB is the institutional mechanism that converts that exposure into accountable practice.

The mathematics of randomization, developed in this chapter, is therefore not only a statistical achievement but an ethical commitment. To randomize is to declare that knowledge is worth seeking and that participants deserve the protections that structured uncertainty demands.
