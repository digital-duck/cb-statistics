What Data Collection Design Achieves

Every theorem in mathematical statistics rests on an implicit assumption: that the numbers fed into an estimator arrived through a principled process. Data collection design is the discipline that makes this assumption true by construction. Formally, a data collection design is a triple $\mathcal{D} = (\Omega, \mathcal{S}, \pi)$, where $\Omega$ is the population or experimental domain, $\mathcal{S} \subseteq 2^{\Omega}$ is the family of admissible samples or treatment assignments, and $\pi : \mathcal{S} \to [0,1]$ is a probability measure encoding how each element of $\mathcal{S}$ is selected. The central achievement of this framework is that it transforms the act of data gathering from an ad hoc procedure into a mathematically tractable object whose properties — bias, variance, identifiability of causal effects — can be derived before a single measurement is taken.

Worked Example: Stratified Sampling

Suppose a population of $N = 1{,}000$ students is partitioned into $H = 3$ strata of sizes $N_1 = 400$, $N_2 = 350$, $N_3 = 250$. We wish to estimate the population mean $\mu$ of exam scores. Under proportional stratified random sampling with total sample size $n = 100$, we allocate $n_h = n \cdot N_h / N$ units per stratum and draw each subsample uniformly without replacement. The resulting estimator

$$\hat{\mu}_{\text{st}} = \sum_{h=1}^{H} \frac{N_h}{N} \bar{y}_h$$

is unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{\text{st}}] = \mu$, and its variance satisfies

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\text{st}}) \;\leq\; \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\text{SRS}})$$

whenever within-stratum variance is smaller than the overall population variance — a guarantee the design encodes structurally, not empirically.

Key Theorem: Design-Unbiasedness

Theorem (Horvitz–Thompson, 1952). Let $\pi_i > 0$ be the inclusion probability of unit $i$ under design $\mathcal{D}$. The estimator $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{HT}} = \sum_{i \in s} y_i / \pi_i$ is unbiased for the population total $T = \sum_{i \in \Omega} y_i$ under every design satisfying positive inclusion probabilities, regardless of the distribution of $y$.

This result is foundational: it decouples inferential validity from modelling assumptions, placing the burden of correctness entirely on the design rather than on parametric hypotheses about the outcome.

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, Rational, summation, simplify

N, n = 1000, 100
strata = {1: 400, 2: 350, 3: 250}
allocations = {h: Rational(n_h, N) * n for h, n_h in strata.items()}
weights = {h: Rational(n_h, N) for h, n_h in strata.items()}
# Verify weights sum to 1
print(simplify(sum(weights.values())))  # → 1

The Natural Endpoint

Data collection design is the natural culmination of a first course in statistical reasoning because it closes the loop between mathematical theory and empirical practice. Every concept encountered earlier — probability spaces, estimator bias, the central limit theorem, hypothesis testing — acquires its operational meaning only once the mechanism generating the data is specified. A poorly designed study can invalidate asymptotically optimal estimators; a well-designed one can yield exact guarantees with modest sample sizes.

As a next step, consider diving into experimental design for causal inference, where the assignment mechanism $\pi$ is under the investigator's direct control. Randomised controlled trials, factorial designs, and response-surface methods each exploit $\mathcal{D}$ in distinct ways to isolate treatment effects from confounding — and the tools developed in this chapter are precisely what makes their analysis rigorous.
