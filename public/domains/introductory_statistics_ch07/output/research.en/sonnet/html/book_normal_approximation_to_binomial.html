What the Normal Approximation Achieves

Throughout this textbook we have built the binomial distribution from first principles: counting successes across $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, each with success probability $p$, to obtain the probability mass function

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}.$$

This formula is exact, but its utility collapses in practice. When $n = 1{,}000$, computing $\binom{1000}{487}$ directly is numerically treacherous, and summing dozens of such terms to evaluate $P(480 \le X \le 520)$ is impractical by hand and unintuitive even with software. The normal approximation dissolves both obstacles simultaneously.

Theorem (De Moivre–Laplace). Let $X \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(n, p)$ with $\mu = np$ and $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)$. Then, as $n \to \infty$,

$$\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1),$$

and for large $n$ with $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$, cumulative probabilities are well approximated by

$$P(a \le X \le b) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{b + \tfrac{1}{2} - \mu}{\sigma}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{a - \tfrac{1}{2} - \mu}{\sigma}\right),$$

where the $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}$ terms constitute the continuity correction that accounts for replacing a discrete sum with a continuous integral.

Worked Example

A fair coin is tossed $n = 400$ times. Let $X$ count the number of heads. Find $P(190 \le X \le 210)$.

Here $\mu = 200$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{400 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5} = 10$. Applying the continuity correction:

$$P(190 \le X \le 210) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{210.5 - 200}{10}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{189.5 - 200}{10}\right) = \Phi(1.05) - \Phi(-1.05) \approx 0.8534 - 0.1469 = 0.706.$$

A direct binomial summation yields $0.703$, confirming the approximation is accurate to three decimal places.

The Bridge to Every Application

This single result is the engine behind an enormous range of quantitative practice. In statistical quality control, it converts defect counts into control-chart thresholds interpretable by factory engineers. In public-opinion polling, it justifies the familiar margin-of-error formula $\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ that appears in every election broadcast. In clinical trials, it underpins the sample-size calculations that regulatory agencies require before a study may begin. In actuarial science, it lets insurers price portfolios of binary risks—policy claims, defaults, equipment failures—without summing millions of binomial terms.
