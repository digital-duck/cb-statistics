What the Normal Approximation Achieves

The binomial distribution $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$ answers a deceptively simple question: how many successes occur in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials? Yet computing $P(X \leq k) = \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n}{j}p^j(1-p)^{n-j}$ becomes numerically treacherous when $n$ exceeds a few hundred — factorials overflow, and the sum contains thousands of terms. The normal approximation resolves this impasse by replacing the discrete histogram with a continuous curve. Specifically, if $np \geq 5$ and $n(1-p) \geq 5$, then

$$\frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

so that $P(a \leq X \leq b) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{b + \tfrac{1}{2} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{a - \tfrac{1}{2} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)$, where the half-integer continuity correction $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}$ accounts for the translation from a discrete to a continuous domain. This result is a direct corollary of the Central Limit Theorem and represents the natural culmination of the course: every tool developed — variance, standardization, the Gaussian integral — converges here.

Worked Example

A quality-control inspector examines $n = 400$ components, each defective with probability $p = 0.08$. What is the probability that fewer than 40 are defective?

The exact mean and standard deviation are $\mu = np = 32$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)} = \sqrt{29.44} \approx 5.426$. Applying the continuity correction,

$$P(X < 40) = P(X \leq 39) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{39.5 - 32}{5.426}\right) = \Phi(1.382) \approx 0.9165.$$

A direct binomial summation yields $0.9175$, confirming the approximation is accurate to within one percentage point.

Key Theorem

De Moivre–Laplace Theorem. Let $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ with $0 < p < 1$ fixed. For any real $a < b$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P\!\left(a \leq \frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \leq b\right) = \int_a^b \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2}\,dt.$$

The proof proceeds by applying Stirling's approximation $\ln(n!) \approx n\ln n - n + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi n)$ to each binomial coefficient and showing the resulting log-probability converges pointwise to the Gaussian log-density.

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, binomial, Rational, N, erf, sqrt, pi

n, p, k = 400, Rational(8, 100), 39
mu = n * p
sigma = sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
z = (k + Rational(1, 2) - mu) / sigma
normal_approx = (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2))) / 2
print("Normal approximation:", N(normal_approx, 6))

The normal approximation to the binomial is not merely a computational shortcut — it is the theorem that first revealed the universality of the bell curve, showing that chance, repeated at scale, always tends toward the same shape.
