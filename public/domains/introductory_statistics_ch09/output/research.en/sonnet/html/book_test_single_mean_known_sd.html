Concept Book: Test Single Mean Known Sd

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the test achieves. Throughout this book we have built, layer by layer, the machinery of probability: random variables, sampling distributions, the Central Limit Theorem, and the standard normal. The one-sample $z$-test for a known population standard deviation — test_single_mean_known_sd — is where that machinery turns into a decision. Given a random sample $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ drawn from a population with known standard deviation $\sigma$, we wish to determine whether the population mean $\mu$ equals a hypothesized value $\mu_0$. The test statistic

$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

follows a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ exactly when $\mu = \mu_0$, by the Central Limit Theorem. Every symbol in this formula has now been earned: $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is the standard error, and the ratio measures how many standard errors separate observation from hypothesis. A $p$-value computed from $|z|$ then tells us the probability of seeing a deviation this large by chance alone.

Worked example. A bottling plant claims its bottles contain $\mu_0 = 500\,\text{mL}$ with known process standard deviation $\sigma = 8\,\text{mL}$. A quality-control inspector draws $n = 64$ bottles and records $\bar{x} = 497.5\,\text{mL}$. The test statistic is

$$z = \frac{497.5 - 500}{8 / \sqrt{64}} = \frac{-2.5}{1} = -2.5.$$

For a two-sided test at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, the critical values are $\pm 1.96$. Since $|{-2.5}| = 2.5 > 1.96$, we reject $H_0$: the evidence suggests the mean fill volume has drifted below specification. The two-sided $p$-value is $2\Phi(-2.5) \approx 0.0124$, confirming a statistically significant finding.

Key theorem. Under $H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$, with $\sigma$ known and $X_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}$ any distribution with finite variance, the statistic $Z = (\bar{X} - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$. For normally distributed populations, this holds for every finite $n$.

Lab cell.

import sympy as sp
from scipy import stats

x_bar, mu_0, sigma, n = 497.5, 500, 8, 64
z = (x_bar - mu_0) / (sigma / n**0.5)
p_value = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))

print(f"z = {z:.4f},  p-value = {p_value:.4f}")
# z = -2.5000,  p-value = 0.0124

An invitation. The bottling example is only one instance of a vast family: clinical trials comparing a new drug's mean biomarker against a historical control, semiconductor fabs monitoring etch-depth uniformity, and educational researchers assessing whether a new curriculum raises mean test scores. Each domain attaches real consequence — regulatory approval, yield loss, student outcomes — to the single question this test answers.
