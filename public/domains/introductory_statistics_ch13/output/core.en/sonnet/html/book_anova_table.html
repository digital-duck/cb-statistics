What the ANOVA Table Achieves

Throughout this text we have built, layer by layer, the machinery required to ask a single deceptively simple question: do the group means differ more than chance alone would predict? The ANOVA table is the instrument that answers it. It partitions the total variation in a response variable $Y$ into explained and unexplained components, assigns degrees of freedom to each, and converts the ratio of mean squares into a single test statistic whose sampling distribution is known exactly under the null hypothesis.

Formally, for a one-way design with $k$ groups and $n$ total observations, the table organises the identity

$$SS_{\text{Total}} = SS_{\text{Between}} + SS_{\text{Within}},$$

where

$$SS_{\text{Between}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i \bigl(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\bigr)^2, \qquad SS_{\text{Within}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}\bigl(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_{i\cdot}\bigr)^2.$$

Dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom ($k-1$ and $n-k$ respectively) yields mean squares $MS_B$ and $MS_W$. The F-statistic

$$F = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W}$$

follows an $F(k-1,\, n-k)$ distribution when the null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_k$ is true and the classical assumptions hold. A large observed $F$ is evidence that at least one group mean departs from the common value.

Why This Is the Natural Endpoint

Every concept introduced in earlier chapters — linear models, sum-of-squares decompositions, the $\chi^2$ and $F$ distributions, ordinary least squares — flows directly into this table. The ANOVA table is not merely a summary; it is the convergence point at which geometry (orthogonal projections in $\mathbb{R}^n$), probability (sampling distributions), and statistical inference (hypothesis testing and confidence regions) meet on a single printed line.

Worked Example

Suppose three teaching methods are applied to groups of sizes $n_1 = n_2 = n_3 = 10$, yielding group means $\bar{Y}_{1\cdot} = 72$, $\bar{Y}_{2\cdot} = 78$, $\bar{Y}_{3\cdot} = 75$, and grand mean $\bar{Y}_{\cdot\cdot} = 75$. If $SS_W = 540$, then

$$SS_B = 10\bigl[(72-75)^2 + (78-75)^2 + (75-75)^2\bigr] = 10[9+9+0] = 180.$$

The completed table reads:

| Source | SS | df | MS | $F$ | |---|---|---|---|---| | Between | 180 | 2 | 90 | $90/20 = 4.50$ | | Within | 540 | 27 | 20 | — | | Total | 720 | 29 | — | — |

At significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ the critical value is $F_{0.05}(2, 27) \approx 3.35$; because $4.50 > 3.35$ we reject $H_0$ and conclude that teaching method affects student scores.

Key Theorem

Fisher's F-ratio theorem. Under $H_0$ and the assumptions of normality, independence, and equal variances, the ratio $MS_B / MS_W$ is distributed exactly as $F(k-1, n-k)$. Consequently the $p$-value computed from the observed $F$ controls the Type I error rate at the nominal level $\alpha$.

Lab Cell

import sympy as sp
from sympy.stats import F as F_dist, cdf

k, n = 3, 30
F_obs = sp.Rational(9, 2)          # 4.50
F_rv  = F_dist("F", k - 1, n - k)
p_value = 1 - cdf(F_rv)(F_obs)
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(p_value))

Running this cell produces the exact symbolic $p$-value, demonstrating that the ANOVA table is not just a numerical recipe but a mathematically precise object amenable to computer algebra.

Invitation

The ANOVA table generalises in every direction that modern data demands: two-way designs add interaction terms, repeated-measures layouts relax independence, and the multivariate extension (MANOVA) replaces $F$ with Wilks' $\Lambda$. Each generalisation preserves the same decomposition logic you have now mastered. Choose any one of these directions — begin with the two-way factorial design — and you will find that the table you built here is still sitting at its centre, unchanged.
