Degrees Of Freedom

In statistics, degrees of freedom ($\text{df}$) is a critically important concept that determines the shape and spread of many probability distributions—most famously the Student's $t$ distribution and the $\chi^2$ distribution. Conceptually, $\text{df}$ does not measure the size of your sample; rather, it counts the number of independent pieces of information available to estimate a parameter. When you use data to calculate a statistic (like the mean or variance), fixing one piece of information inevitably reduces the freedom you have in estimating another. If you know the total height of five people and four of their heights, the fifth person’s height is fixed—you only had three degrees of freedom for those estimates.

Consider calculating the sample variance ($s^2$). The formula requires us to first calculate the sample mean ($\bar{x}$). Once $\bar{x}$ is determined from $n$ data points, every single data point must satisfy the relationship that its distance from the mean contributes to the overall sum of squares. Because $\text{df} = n-1$, we lose one degree of freedom when calculating $s^2$: once you know the sample mean, all individual observations are constrained by it.
