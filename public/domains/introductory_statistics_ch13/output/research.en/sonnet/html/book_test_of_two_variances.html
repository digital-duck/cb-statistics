Concept Book: Test Of Two Variances

Introductory Statistics Ch13 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The test of two variances addresses the following question: given independent random samples from two populations, is there sufficient statistical evidence to conclude that the populations differ in their variability? This is distinct from comparing means—two manufacturing processes may produce components with identical average diameters yet wildly different spread, and it is the spread that determines whether a part fits. Formalizing the question requires a ratio of sample variances, which, under normality, follows a known theoretical distribution.

Definition. Let $X_1, \ldots, X_{n_1} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_2} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ be independent samples. The test statistic

$$F = \frac{S_1^2 / \sigma_1^2}{S_2^2 / \sigma_2^2}$$

follows an $F$-distribution with $(n_1 - 1, n_2 - 1)$ degrees of freedom. Under the null hypothesis $H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$, the unknown population variances cancel, yielding the observable ratio $F = S_1^2 / S_2^2$.

Worked example. A pharmaceutical laboratory records tablet-dissolution times (seconds) from two production lines. Line A yields $n_1 = 16$ tablets with $S_1^2 = 24.3$; Line B yields $n_2 = 21$ tablets with $S_2^2 = 9.7$. The observed statistic is $F_{\text{obs}} = 24.3 / 9.7 \approx 2.505$. The two-tailed $p$-value is computed from the $F(15, 20)$ distribution. If $p < 0.05$, the laboratory concludes that the lines differ in consistency—a regulatory finding, not merely an operational one.

Key theorem (Fisher, 1924). If $U \sim \chi^2(d_1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2(d_2)$ are independent, then $F = (U/d_1)/(V/d_2) \sim F(d_1, d_2)$. Since $(n_i - 1)S_i^2/\sigma_i^2 \sim \chi^2(n_i - 1)$, the $F$-ratio emerges as a direct corollary—the entire test rests on this chi-squared decomposition of sample variance.

Lab cell.

from scipy import stats
F_obs = 24.3 / 9.7
df1, df2 = 15, 20
p_value = 2 * min(stats.f.cdf(F_obs, df1, df2),
                  1 - stats.f.cdf(F_obs, df1, df2))
print(f"F = {F_obs:.4f}, p = {p_value:.4f}")

The test of two variances is the natural endpoint of this text because it synthesizes every prior concept—sampling distributions, the chi-squared family, degrees of freedom, and hypothesis testing logic—into a single inferential act. Its reach is broad: quality engineers use it to certify process equivalence before a production changeover; clinical statisticians apply it to verify the equal-variance assumption that underpins the two-sample $t$-test; financial analysts compare the volatility of two asset classes to assess portfolio risk. Each domain transforms the same mathematical object into a domain-specific decision.

You are now equipped to pursue any of these directions. The deepest immediate return lies in pharmaceutical quality control, where variance testing is embedded in regulatory frameworks such as ICH Q6A. Begin there, and you will see this theorem become policy.
