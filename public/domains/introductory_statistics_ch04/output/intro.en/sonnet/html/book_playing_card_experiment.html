Payoff

Throughout this text we have built probability theory from the ground up: sample spaces, events, measures, conditional probability, independence, and combinatorial counting. The playing-card experiment — drawing one or more cards from a well-shuffled standard deck of 52 — is the natural capstone because it forces every one of those ideas to work simultaneously in a single, completely specified model.

What the experiment achieves. A standard deck supplies a finite, uniform probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ in which $|\Omega| = \binom{52}{k}$ for a hand of $k$ cards drawn without replacement, and $P(\{\omega\}) = 1/|\Omega|$ for every elementary outcome $\omega$. This uniformity means every abstract formula we have derived admits an exact rational answer — no approximation, no asymptotic argument. The experiment therefore serves as a proof of concept: if the theory is correct, its predictions must match the counts we can verify by hand or by exhaustive computer enumeration.

Connecting the threads. Computing the probability of a flush (five cards of the same suit) requires the multiplication principle and combinations:

$$P(\text{flush}) = \frac{4\binom{13}{5}}{\binom{52}{5}} = \frac{5148}{2{,}598{,}960} \approx 0.00198.$$

Asking whether the event "first card is an ace" and "second card is a heart" are independent introduces conditional probability and reveals that sampling without replacement destroys independence — a fact with direct consequences in survey sampling and cryptographic protocols. Computing the expected number of suits represented in a five-card hand exercises linearity of expectation via indicator random variables $X_i = \mathbf{1}[\text{suit }i\text{ appears}]$, giving $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^4 X_i] = 4\left(1 - \binom{39}{5}/\binom{52}{5}\right) \approx 3.15$. Each calculation is a direct application of a theorem proved in an earlier chapter, now unified under one experiment.

An invitation. The deepest thread to pull is the connection between card hands and combinatorial design theory. The 13 ranks and 4 suits are not arbitrary: they form a structure closely related to finite projective planes and Latin squares, objects that underpin error-correcting codes used in every wireless transmission you send. If you wish to see how a deck of cards secretly encodes the mathematics of digital communication, the next chapter awaits.
