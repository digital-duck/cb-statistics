What the Interquartile Range of the Normal Distribution Achieves

The interquartile range of the normal distribution (IQR$_{\mathcal{N}}$) is the length of the interval that captures the central half of all probability mass under a Gaussian curve. For a random variable $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the first and third quartiles are $Q_1 = \mu - z_{0.75}\,\sigma$ and $Q_3 = \mu + z_{0.75}\,\sigma$, where $z_{0.75} \approx 0.6745$ is the upper $25\%$ point of the standard normal. The interquartile range is therefore

$$\mathrm{IQR}_{\mathcal{N}} = Q_3 - Q_1 = 2 \times 0.6745\,\sigma \approx 1.3490\,\sigma.$$

This single formula is the natural endpoint of the journey through normal-distribution theory because it fuses two previously separate ideas — the geometry of the bell curve and the order statistics of a symmetric distribution — into one closed-form relationship. Unlike the variance $\sigma^2$, the IQR is expressed in the same units as $X$ itself and is entirely unaffected by the location parameter $\mu$. Unlike the mean absolute deviation, it admits an exact analytic expression under the Gaussian assumption, making algebraic manipulation straightforward.

Worked Example

Suppose examination scores follow $X \sim \mathcal{N}(70, 100)$, so $\mu = 70$ and $\sigma = 10$.

$$Q_1 = 70 - 0.6745 \times 10 = 63.255, \qquad Q_3 = 70 + 0.6745 \times 10 = 76.745.$$ $$\mathrm{IQR}_{\mathcal{N}} = 76.745 - 63.255 = 13.490.$$

Exactly half the students score between $63.3$ and $76.7$ points. An examiner can immediately interpret the spread without computing a square root of a variance or consulting a separate table.

Key Theorem: Scale Equivariance

If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $Y = aX + b$ for constants $a > 0$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$, then

$$\mathrm{IQR}(Y) = a \cdot \mathrm{IQR}(X).$$

Proof sketch. Since $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu + b,\, a^2\sigma^2)$, the quartiles of $Y$ are $a Q_1 + b$ and $a Q_3 + b$. Subtracting eliminates $b$, yielding $a(Q_3 - Q_1)$. $\square$

This scale-equivariance property is precisely what makes IQR$_{\mathcal{N}}$ a robust scale estimator: the shift $b$ is invisible to it, and a rescaling propagates linearly rather than quadratically as it would for the variance.

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy import symbols, sqrt, pi, exp, Rational, erfinv, simplify, N

sigma = symbols('sigma', positive=True)
# Exact symbolic IQR for N(0, sigma^2)
z_75 = sqrt(2) * erfinv(Rational(1, 2))          # erfinv(0.5) = z_{0.75}/sqrt(2)
IQR_exact = 2 * sqrt(2) * erfinv(Rational(1, 2)) * sigma
print("Exact IQR =", simplify(IQR_exact))
print("Numerical coefficient:", N(2 * sqrt(2) * erfinv(Rational(1, 2)), 6))

Running this cell confirms the coefficient $1.34898\ldots$, matching the analytical result to six significant figures.

Invitation

The relationship $\mathrm{IQR}_{\mathcal{N}} \approx 1.3490\,\sigma$ is more than a curiosity about one distribution. It is the foundation of robust scale estimation: by inverting the formula, $\hat{\sigma} = \mathrm{IQR}_{\mathrm{sample}} / 1.3490$, one obtains an estimator of $\sigma$ that resists contamination by outliers far more effectively than the sample standard deviation. If you wish to see how this single formula reshapes the entire field of robust statistics — from Tukey's fences to the median absolute deviation to modern high-breakdown estimators — the next chapter awaits.
