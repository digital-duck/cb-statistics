What the Experiment Achieves

The playing-card experiment is a deliberately minimal yet structurally complete probability space. A standard deck of 52 cards supplies a finite sample space $\Omega$ with $|\Omega| = 52$, a natural $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F} = 2^{\Omega}$, and a uniform probability measure $\mathbb{P}(\{s\}) = \tfrac{1}{52}$ for every singleton $\{s\} \in \mathcal{F}$. Every abstract axiom introduced earlier in this book — non-negativity, normalization, countable additivity — can be verified by direct count, making the deck the canonical "first laboratory" in which abstract measure theory becomes concrete arithmetic.

More than a pedagogical convenience, the experiment is structurally universal. Because $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is a discrete uniform space, it serves as the prototype from which all finite probability spaces are built by re-weighting or by partitioning $\Omega$ into equivalence classes. The two-dimensional index set — rank $r \in \{A, 2, \ldots, K\}$ and suit $s \in \{\clubsuit, \diamondsuit, \heartsuit, \spadesuit\}$ — forces the student to distinguish marginal distributions from joint distributions and to recognize independence as a structural property, not a mere numerical coincidence.

A Worked Illustration

Draw one card uniformly at random. Define events $R = \{\text{card is a red suit}\}$ and $K = \{\text{card is a King}\}$.

$$\mathbb{P}(R) = \frac{26}{52} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(K) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(R \cap K) = \frac{2}{52} = \frac{1}{26}.$$

Since $\mathbb{P}(R \cap K) = \mathbb{P}(R)\,\mathbb{P}(K)$, the events are independent. The argument generalizes immediately: any rank event and any color event are independent in the uniform measure, because rank and suit are orthogonal coordinates on $\Omega$.

Key Theorem

Theorem (Product structure of the uniform deck measure). Let $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{S}$ denote the $\sigma$-algebras generated by rank and suit respectively. Under the uniform measure $\mathbb{P}$, $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{S}$ are independent $\sigma$-algebras; that is, for every $A \in \mathcal{R}$ and $B \in \mathcal{S}$,

$$\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A)\,\mathbb{P}(B).$$

Proof sketch. Any $A \in \mathcal{R}$ is a union of $|A_r|$ rank classes, each of size 4; any $B \in \mathcal{S}$ is a union of $|B_s|$ suit classes, each of size 13. Their intersection contains exactly $|A_r| \cdot |B_s|$ cards, so $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) = \tfrac{4|A_r|}{52} \cdot \tfrac{13|B_s|}{52} \cdot 52/52$; cancellation yields the product formula. $\square$

Lab Cell

from sympy import Rational, symbols
from sympy.stats import DiscreteUniform, P, E

# Build the 52-card space symbolically
suits = ['C','D','H','S']
ranks = list(range(1, 14))
deck  = [f"{r}{s}" for r in ranks for s in suits]

X = DiscreteUniform('X', deck)

# P(red) and P(King) and their product
p_red  = P(X.pspace.domain.set.count(lambda c: c[-1] in ['D','H']) / 52)
# Direct rational arithmetic
p_R  = Rational(26, 52)
p_K  = Rational(4,  52)
p_RK = Rational(2,  52)

print(p_R, p_K, p_RK, p_R * p_K == p_RK)   # True → independence confirmed
