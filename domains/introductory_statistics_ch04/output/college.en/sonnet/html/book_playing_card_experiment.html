Concept Book: Playing Card Experiment

Payoff

Throughout this chapter we have built, layer by layer, the machinery of classical probability: sample spaces, events, equally-likely outcomes, and the combinatorial tools—permutations, combinations, the multiplication principle—needed to count them exactly. The playing-card experiment is the natural capstone because a standard 52-card deck is, in effect, a perfectly engineered probability laboratory. Every fundamental idea we have developed finds a clean, concrete instantiation inside a single shuffled deck.

What the experiment achieves. Draw $k$ cards without replacement from a deck of $N = 52$ cards containing $K$ cards of a distinguished type (say, aces, hearts, or face cards). The sample space

$$\Omega = \binom{N}{k}$$

is finite and uniform, so every elementary outcome is equally likely. The number of favorable outcomes—exactly $m$ distinguished cards among the $k$ drawn—is

$$|\{A_m\}| = \binom{K}{m}\binom{N-K}{k-m},$$

giving the hypergeometric probability

$$P(X = m) = \frac{\displaystyle\binom{K}{m}\binom{N-K}{k-m}}{\displaystyle\binom{N}{k}}, \qquad m = 0, 1, \ldots, \min(k, K).$$

This single formula unifies counting, combinatorics, and probability into one coherent object. Its derivation exercises every technique of the chapter, yet the answer is simple enough to compute by hand for small $k$.

Key theorem (Hypergeometric expectation). If $X$ counts the distinguished cards in a draw of $k$ from a population of $N$ containing $K$ successes, then

$$E[X] = k \cdot \frac{K}{N}.$$

The proof follows from linearity of expectation: write $X = X_1 + \cdots + X_k$ where $X_i = 1$ if the $i$-th card drawn is distinguished, and observe that $P(X_i = 1) = K/N$ by symmetry. The variance is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = k\,\frac{K}{N}\,\frac{N-K}{N}\,\frac{N-k}{N-1}$, where the factor $(N-k)/(N-1)$ is the finite-population correction, distinguishing sampling without replacement from the simpler binomial model.

Lab cell (SymPy).

from sympy import binomial, Rational, symbols, simplify

N, K, k, m = 52, 13, 5, 2          # e.g. hearts in a 5-card hand
prob = Rational(binomial(K, m) * binomial(N - K, k - m),
                binomial(N, k))
print(f"P(X = {m}) = {prob} ≈ {float(prob):.4f}")

Run this cell, then vary $K$ and $k$ to watch how the distribution shifts. The playing-card experiment is not merely a textbook exercise: its exact combinatorial structure recurs in quality-control sampling, ecological mark-recapture studies, and the analysis of clinical trials with small cohorts. Each of those fields inherits directly from the formula above. Pick any one of them—mark-recapture ecology is particularly striking—and carry the hypergeometric model into its natural habitat.
