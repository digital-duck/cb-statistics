Payoff

Throughout this text we have assembled, piece by piece, the machinery of distance-weighted regression: least-squares minimisation, the geometry of residuals, metric selection, and the translation of physical intuition into computable loss functions. The concept regression\_labs\_distance\_textbook\_fuel names the moment those pieces lock together into a single, self-contained workflow — one in which a student can take raw fuel-consumption measurements from a lab bench, choose a distance functional appropriate to the noise structure of the data, fit a regression model, and interpret the fitted coefficients in physical units. It is the natural endpoint because it is the first time every prerequisite is simultaneously in play: calculus supplies the optimality conditions, linear algebra supplies the solution, geometry supplies the meaning, and the physical domain supplies the ground truth against which the model is tested.

Worked example. Suppose a laboratory experiment records engine load $x_i \in [0,1]$ and fuel flow $y_i$ (in mL s$^{-1}$) for $n = 30$ trials. We minimise the weighted squared-distance loss

$$ L(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \left(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\right)^2, \quad w_i = \sigma_i^{-2}, $$

where $\sigma_i^2$ is the known measurement variance at trial $i$. The ordinary normal equations become the weighted normal equations $(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{X})\,\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{y}$, whose solution $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{y}$ is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator under heteroscedastic Gaussian noise (Gauss–Markov for weighted regression).

Key theorem. If $\mathbf{W} = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^{-2}, \ldots, \sigma_n^{-2})$ and $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p$, then $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ achieves the minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimators, with covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{W} \mathbf{X})^{-1}$.

Lab cell.

import sympy as sp

beta0, beta1 = sp.symbols('beta0 beta1')
x = [0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0]
y = [2.1, 3.8, 5.5, 7.2]
w = [1.0, 0.5, 1.0, 0.5]

L = sum(w[i]*(y[i] - beta0 - beta1*x[i])**2 for i in range(4))
sol = sp.solve([sp.diff(L, beta0), sp.diff(L, beta1)], [beta0, beta1])
print(sol)

The model fitted here is not merely an exercise: it is the prototype for energy-consumption forecasting, engine-efficiency certification, and any domain where a physical rate (fuel, heat, charge) varies linearly with a control input and measurements carry unequal reliability. You now hold every tool needed to pursue one of those domains in full rigour. The chapter that follows invites you to choose your application, sharpen your distance functional to match its noise signature, and discover what the data have been trying to say all along.
