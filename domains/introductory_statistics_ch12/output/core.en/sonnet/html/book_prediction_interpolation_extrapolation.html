Prediction, Interpolation, and Extrapolation

Every quantitative discipline reduces, at some point, to a single imperative: given what we know, estimate what we do not. The concept of prediction via interpolation and extrapolation formalizes this imperative. Given a function $f$ known only at a finite set of nodes $\{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ with values $\{y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_n\}$, we construct a surrogate $\hat{f}$ that agrees with $f$ at the nodes and assigns meaningful values elsewhere. When $\hat{f}$ is evaluated at a point $x^$ lying between the known nodes, the process is called interpolation; when $x^$ lies outside the convex hull of the nodes, the process is called extrapolation. Both are instances of the general prediction problem, differentiated only by the epistemic risk each carries.

Worked Example. Suppose a student records the temperature $T$ (in ${}^\circ$C) of a cooling object at three instants:

$$ (t_0, T_0) = (0,\, 80),\quad (t_1, T_1) = (5,\, 55),\quad (t_2, T_2) = (10,\, 38). $$

The unique quadratic polynomial through these points is found via the Lagrange formula

$$ \hat{T}(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{2} T_k \prod_{\substack{j=0 \\ j \neq k}}^{2} \frac{t - t_j}{t_k - t_j}. $$

Evaluating at $t^ = 7$ (interpolation) yields $\hat{T}(7) \approx 46.0$, a value we never measured but can trust within the interval. Evaluating at $t^ = 20$ (extrapolation) yields a number that must be treated with caution: the surrogate polynomial has no physical reason to track the true cooling curve beyond the observed window.

Key Theorem (Interpolation Error). If $f \in C^{n+1}[a, b]$ and $\hat{f}$ is the degree-$n$ interpolating polynomial through $n+1$ distinct nodes in $[a, b]$, then for every $x \in [a, b]$ there exists $\xi \in (a, b)$ such that

$$ f(x) - \hat{f}(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\,\prod_{k=0}^{n}(x - x_k). $$

This bound quantifies the interpolation promise: increasing $n$ or choosing nodes wisely (e.g., Chebyshev nodes) drives the error toward zero inside the interval, while no analogous bound governs the extrapolation regime.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import symbols, interpolate, Rational, lambdify
t = symbols('t')
data = [(0, 80), (5, 55), (10, 38)]
p = interpolate(data, t)
print("Polynomial:", p)
print("Interpolated T(7)  =", p.subs(t, 7).evalf(4))
print("Extrapolated T(20) =", p.subs(t, 20).evalf(4))
