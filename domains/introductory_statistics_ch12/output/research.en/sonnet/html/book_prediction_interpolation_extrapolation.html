What the Concept Achieves

Every quantitative discipline reduces, at its core, to the same question: given what we have measured, what can we say about what we have not? Prediction, interpolation, and extrapolation are the three answers to that question, distinguished by where the unknown point lies relative to the data.

Let $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a set of observations with $x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_n$. We seek a function $\hat{f}$ such that $\hat{f}(x_i) \approx y_i$ for all $i$, and more importantly, $\hat{f}(x^)$ is a useful estimate at some new input $x^$. When $x^ \in [x_1, x_n]$ the operation is interpolation; when $x^ \notin [x_1, x_n]$ it is extrapolation; when $x^*$ represents a future or unobserved condition governed by a model, the broader term prediction applies. These are not three separate techniques — they are one unified framework, and this chapter has built every tool needed to operate it.

A Worked Example

Suppose temperature readings (in °C) are recorded at hours $t \in \{0, 2, 4, 6\}$:

$$T(0)=15,\quad T(2)=18,\quad T(4)=21,\quad T(6)=19.$$

A cubic spline through these four points yields $\hat{T}(t)$ that is twice continuously differentiable. Evaluating $\hat{T}(3)$ is interpolation; evaluating $\hat{T}(8)$ is extrapolation; fitting a sinusoidal model $T(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) + C$ and reading off tomorrow's noon value is prediction in the full modelling sense. The same fitted object $\hat{f}$ serves all three purposes.

The Fundamental Theorem of Polynomial Interpolation

Theorem. Given $n+1$ distinct nodes $x_0,\ldots,x_n$ and values $y_0,\ldots,y_n$, there exists a unique polynomial $p \in \mathcal{P}_n$ such that $p(x_i)=y_i$ for all $i$. The interpolation error at $x^*$ satisfies

$$f(x^*) - p(x^*) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x^*-x_i)$$

for some $\xi$ in the convex hull of $\{x_0,\ldots,x_n,x^*\}$.

This error bound makes the risk of extrapolation visible: outside the data range the product $\prod(x^*-x_i)$ grows without bound, so even a small $(n+1)$-th derivative inflates the error dramatically.

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, interpolate, plot

t = symbols('t')
data = {0: 15, 2: 18, 4: 21, 6: 19}
p = interpolate(list(data.items()), t)
print("Interpolating polynomial:", p)
print("Interpolation  T(3) =", p.subs(t, 3))
print("Extrapolation  T(8) =", p.subs(t, 8))
plot(p, (t, -1, 9), title="Cubic interpolant with extrapolation")

Running this cell makes the divergence of the extrapolated tail visible — a computational confirmation of the theorem above.
