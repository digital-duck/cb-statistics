Payoff

Throughout this text we have built the machinery of linear regression piece by piece — least-squares minimisation, the normal equations, coefficient interpretation, and residual analysis. The concept regression\_labs\_distance\_textbook\_fuel draws every thread together: it is the applied problem of modelling fuel consumption as a smooth, predictable function of distance driven, and it is the natural capstone because it forces you to exercise every prior tool in a single, physically meaningful setting.

Formally, let $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a dataset where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ denotes the distance (km) of the $i$-th trip and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ its fuel consumption (litres). The simple linear model posits

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),$$

and the ordinary-least-squares estimators

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$$

yield the unique minimiser of the residual sum of squares $\text{RSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$.

Worked example. Suppose five trips yield the pairs $(10, 1.1)$, $(25, 2.6)$, $(40, 3.9)$, $(55, 5.4)$, $(70, 7.0)$ (distance in km, fuel in litres). Computing $\bar{x} = 40$, $\bar{y} = 4.0$, one obtains $\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 0.098\ \text{L km}^{-1}$ and $\hat{\beta}_0 \approx 0.08\ \text{L}$. The slope is interpretable directly: each additional kilometre requires roughly $98\ \text{mL}$ of fuel.

Key theorem (Gauss–Markov). Among all linear unbiased estimators of $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, the OLS estimators have minimum variance. This guarantees that the fitted line extracted from lab data is, in a precise sense, the best linear summary the data can support.

import sympy as sp

x_data = [10, 25, 40, 55, 70]
y_data = [1.1, 2.6, 3.9, 5.4, 7.0]

n   = len(x_data)
x_m = sum(x_data) / n
y_m = sum(y_data) / n

b1  = sum((x - x_m)*(y - y_m) for x, y in zip(x_data, y_data)) \
    / sum((x - x_m)**2 for x in x_data)
b0  = y_m - b1 * x_m

b0_sym, b1_sym = sp.Rational(b0).limit_denominator(1000), \
                 sp.Rational(b1).limit_denominator(1000)
print(f"ŷ = {b0_sym} + {b1_sym} · x")

This single model unlocks a wide range of consequential problems. In transportation engineering, it underpins fuel-budget forecasting for fleet operators. In environmental science, it converts route-distance distributions directly into carbon-emission estimates via the chain $\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$ combined with an emissions factor. In urban planning, the slope $\hat{\beta}_1$ informs the marginal cost of sprawl: every kilometre added to the average commute has a calculable fuel penalty that planners can monetise and compare against transit investment. In automotive testing, controlled lab measurements of the same vehicle under varying distances produce the calibration curve that manufacturers report as the official consumption rating.

The payoff, then, is not merely a fitted line. It is the realisation that a two-parameter model, derived from first principles and verified by the Gauss–Markov theorem, is sufficient to inform decisions at scales ranging from a single household's monthly fuel budget to a nation's transport-emissions policy.

You are now equipped to pursue any of these directions with rigour.
