Payoff

By the time a student arrives at regression through the lens of distance, three previously separate threads — geometric intuition, optimization theory, and empirical data analysis — converge into a single coherent framework. The concept captured by regression\_labs\_distance\_textbook\_fuel is precisely this convergence: ordinary least-squares regression reinterpreted as the problem of minimizing a squared Euclidean distance in a high-dimensional vector space, grounded throughout by fuel-consumption data that a student can measure, manipulate, and challenge.

What has been achieved. Begin with the formal statement. Given an $n \times p$ design matrix $X$ and a response vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the least-squares estimator

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\arg\min}\; \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$$

is the coefficient vector whose image $X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space $\mathcal{C}(X)$. The residual vector $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is therefore perpendicular to every column of $X$, a fact expressible as $X^\top \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}$. Distance, in this reading, is not a metaphor — it is the objective function itself.

Worked example. Consider fuel consumption (in L/100 km) for $n = 50$ vehicles, with predictors engine displacement $x_1$ and vehicle mass $x_2$. Forming $X = [\mathbf{1},\, \mathbf{x}_1,\, \mathbf{x}_2]$ and computing $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y}$ yields, say, $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.043$ (L per cm$^3$) and $\hat{\beta}_2 = 0.0021$ (L per kg). The residual sum of squares $\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2$ measures precisely how far the fitted plane lies from the observed responses — a distance in $\mathbb{R}^{50}$.

Key theorem (Gauss–Markov). Among all linear unbiased estimators, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ achieves the smallest variance for every linear combination $\mathbf{c}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}$, provided the errors have zero mean and constant variance $\sigma^2 I$. The proof proceeds by writing any competing estimator as $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (C + D)\mathbf{y}$ with $DX = 0$, then showing $\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) - \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \sigma^2 DD^\top \succeq 0$.

Lab cell.

import sympy as sp

X, y = sp.Matrix([[1,2],[1,4],[1,6]]), sp.Matrix([3,5,8])
beta_hat = (X.T * X).inv() * X.T * y
r = y - X * beta_hat
sp.pprint({"beta_hat": beta_hat, "||r||^2": r.dot(r)})

Run this cell, then replace the toy data with your fuel dataset. Notice that the norm $\|\mathbf{r}\|^2$ decreases monotonically as you add physically meaningful predictors — each new column reduces the distance between $\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathcal{C}(X)$.

Invitation. The fuel domain is only the entry point. Every field that records measurements and seeks parsimonious explanation — from pharmacokinetics to structural engineering to climate modelling — reduces, at its mathematical core, to this same distance-minimization problem. Choose one such domain, reformulate its governing equations as a design matrix, and you will discover that the geometry you have just mastered is already waiting there, unchanged.
