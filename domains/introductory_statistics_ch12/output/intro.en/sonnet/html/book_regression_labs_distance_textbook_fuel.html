What has been built. Throughout this textbook, you have assembled the three pillars of applied regression: a geometric understanding of distance as a loss criterion, a symbolic toolkit for deriving closed-form estimators, and a laboratory workflow for validating those estimators against real data. The concept regression_labs_distance_textbook_fuel is the point at which all three pillars bear weight simultaneously. A fuel-consumption dataset — miles driven against litres consumed, or kilometres against kilojoules expended — is deceptively simple, yet it exercises every idea developed in the preceding chapters.

Why it is the natural endpoint. Fuel consumption obeys an affine relationship with distance to a first approximation: if $d$ denotes distance (km) and $f$ denotes fuel used (L), then

$$f = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),$$

where $\beta_1$ is the vehicle's marginal consumption rate (L km$^{-1}$) and $\beta_0$ captures engine warm-up overhead. The ordinary least-squares estimators

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_i - \bar{d})(f_i - \bar{f})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_i - \bar{d})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{f} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{d}$$

are precisely the minimisers of the Euclidean residual norm $\|\mathbf{f} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$, connecting the abstract distance geometry of Chapter 3 to a quantity a motorist can measure at a petrol station.

Key theorem (Gauss–Markov, applied). Under the assumptions of the linear model, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE): among all linear unbiased estimators, it achieves minimum variance. For the fuel model this means that no competing linear rule can produce tighter confidence intervals on $\beta_1$ — the rate at which the vehicle consumes fuel per kilometre — given the same observations.

Worked example. Suppose five recorded trips yield $(d_i, f_i)$ pairs (km, L): $(10, 0.9),\,(25, 2.1),\,(40, 3.4),\,(60, 5.0),\,(80, 6.8)$. Computing $\bar{d} = 43$, $\bar{f} = 3.64$, one obtains $\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 0.083$ L km$^{-1}$ and $\hat{\beta}_0 \approx 0.07$ L, predicting roughly 8.3 L per 100 km with a negligible cold-start cost — consistent with a modern compact car.

from sympy import symbols, Rational, simplify
d, f = symbols('d f')
d_vals = [10, 25, 40, 60, 80]
f_vals = [Rational(9,10), Rational(21,10),
          Rational(34,10), Rational(50,10), Rational(68,10)]
n = len(d_vals)
d_bar = sum(d_vals) / n
f_bar = sum(f_vals) / n
num = sum((di - d_bar)*(fi - f_bar) for di,fi in zip(d_vals, f_vals))
den = sum((di - d_bar)**2 for di in d_vals)
beta1 = simplify(num / den)
beta0 = simplify(f_bar - beta1 * d_bar)
print(f"β₁ = {beta1}  β₀ = {beta0}")

Invitation. The fuel-distance model is a prototype for an entire family of consumption problems: battery discharge versus kilometres in electric vehicles, caloric expenditure versus steps walked, CO$_2$ emission versus tonne-kilometres in freight logistics. Each replaces $(d, f)$ with a domain-specific pair yet inherits every theorem proved here. Choose one of these domains, collect or simulate twenty observations, and derive the least-squares fit from first principles — you will find that the mathematics requires no modification, only the physical interpretation changes.
