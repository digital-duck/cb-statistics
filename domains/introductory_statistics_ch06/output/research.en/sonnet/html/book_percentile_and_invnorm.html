What the Inverse Normal Achieves

Throughout this text we have built the machinery of probability densities, cumulative distribution functions, and standardization. The concept of the percentile under a normal distribution—and its computational inverse, the inverse normal function—is the point at which all of that machinery becomes actionable. A percentile answers the question what fraction of the population lies below this value? The inverse normal answers the converse: given a fraction $p \in (0,1)$, what value $x$ has exactly that fraction of the distribution below it?

Definition. Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The $p$-th percentile of $X$ is the unique value $x_p$ satisfying

$$P(X \le x_p) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x_p - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = p,$$

where $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF. Equivalently,

$$x_p = \mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p).$$

The function $\Phi^{-1}$ is the quantile function (also called the inverse normal or norminv); it exists and is strictly increasing because $\Phi$ is a continuous, strictly increasing bijection from $\mathbb{R}$ to $(0,1)$.

Worked Example. Adult resting heart rates follow $X \sim \mathcal{N}(70, 100)$ (mean 70 bpm, $\sigma = 10$). Find the 90th percentile.

$$x_{0.90} = 70 + 10 \cdot \Phi^{-1}(0.90) \approx 70 + 10 \times 1.2816 = 82.82 \text{ bpm.}$$

Any patient recording above $82.82$ bpm falls in the top 10% of the reference population—a clinically meaningful threshold derived in two lines of arithmetic.

Key Theorem (Monotone Transformation of Quantiles). If $Y = g(X)$ for a strictly increasing function $g$, then the $p$-th quantile of $Y$ equals $g(x_p)$. This guarantees that log-normal quantiles, Box–Cox-transformed quantiles, and similar derived quantities inherit their tractability directly from $\Phi^{-1}$.

Connection to the Interquartile Range

The most immediate application is the interquartile range of a normal distribution. By definition, $\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = x_{0.75} - x_{0.25}$. Applying the formula above:

$$\mathrm{IQR} = \sigma\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(0.75) - \Phi^{-1}(0.25)\bigr) = \sigma \times 2 \times 0.6745 \approx 1.3490\,\sigma.$$

This single identity converts a robust spread statistic—one that ignores the tails entirely—into a direct estimate of $\sigma$, forming the backbone of the normalized IQR used in outlier detection and robust regression. Without $\Phi^{-1}$, the connection between $Q_1$, $Q_3$, and $\sigma$ would remain numerically opaque.

from sympy import symbols, stats, N
from sympy.stats import Normal, cdf, quantile

X = Normal('X', mu=0, sigma=1)
Q1 = quantile(X)(0.25)
Q3 = quantile(X)(0.75)
iqr_coefficient = N(Q3 - Q1, 6)   # ≈ 1.34898
print(f"IQR / σ  =  {iqr_coefficient}")

An Invitation

You now possess the key that unlocks distributional thresholds across every quantitative discipline—clinical reference ranges, financial value-at-risk, quality-control specification limits, and psychometric score norming all reduce to evaluating $\Phi^{-1}$ at a chosen probability level. To see the power in concentrated form, turn to the next chapter, where we derive the closed-form IQR of the normal distribution and use it to build a robust estimator of $\sigma$ that resists contamination by even 25% outliers—an estimator first proposed by Gauss and rediscovered by modern data scientists under the name of the normalized IQR.
