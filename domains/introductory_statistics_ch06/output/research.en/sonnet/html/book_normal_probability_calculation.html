What Normal Probability Calculation Achieves

Every thread drawn through this textbook — from the definition of a random variable, through the construction of the Gaussian density, to the mechanics of standardization — converges on a single practical act: computing the probability that a normally distributed quantity falls within a specified range. Formally, given $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the central object is

$$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)dx = \Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right),$$

where $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Because $\Phi$ lacks a closed form in elementary functions, its numerical evaluation — via series expansion, continued fractions, or the complementary error function $\operatorname{erfc}$ — is the computational capstone that makes the entire theory actionable.

This is the natural endpoint because the normal distribution is not merely convenient: the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that sums of independent, finite-variance random variables converge in distribution to it, regardless of the summands' own shape. Mastering $\Phi$ therefore gives the student a single tool that reaches across nearly every quantitative discipline.

Key Theorem

Central Limit Theorem (statement). Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Then

$$\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

The proof proceeds by showing that the characteristic function of the standardized sum converges pointwise to $e^{-t^2/2}$, the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Lévy's continuity theorem then promotes convergence of characteristic functions to convergence in distribution. What matters practically is the corollary: for large $n$, every probability statement about $\bar{X}_n$ reduces to an evaluation of $\Phi$.

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, erf, sqrt, Rational, N, pi, exp, oo
from sympy.stats import Normal, P, E

X = Normal('X', mu=0, sigma=1)          # standard normal
prob = P((X > -1) & (X < 2))           # P(-1 < X < 2)
print("Exact:", prob)
print("Numerical:", N(prob, 10))

Running this cell produces $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-1) \approx 0.8186$, a number that appears unchanged whether the underlying quantity is the average weight of 200 ball bearings, the return on a diversified portfolio over a trading day, or the displacement of a Brownian particle after one second. The universality is the payoff.
