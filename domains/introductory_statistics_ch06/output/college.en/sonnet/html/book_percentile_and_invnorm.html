What This Concept Achieves

Throughout this chapter we have moved steadily from raw data toward a precise language for measuring position. The concept of the percentile and inverse normal transformation is the natural endpoint of that journey because it closes a loop: having learned to compute probabilities forward (from a value $x$ to a tail area $p$), we now invert the map and ask, given a probability $p$, what value $x$ produces it?

Formally, let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The $p$-th percentile of $X$ is the unique value $x_p$ satisfying

$$P(X \leq x_p) = p, \qquad p \in (0, 1).$$

Because the standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi$ is strictly increasing and continuous, its inverse $\Phi^{-1}$ exists everywhere on $(0,1)$. For the general normal distribution the percentile is

$$x_p = \mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p).$$

This single formula is the master key: every question of the form "what score separates the top $k\%$ from the rest?" reduces to one evaluation of $\Phi^{-1}$.

Worked example. Suppose exam scores follow $\mathcal{N}(72, 8^2)$ and an instructor wishes to set the passing threshold at the 30th percentile. We compute $\Phi^{-1}(0.30) \approx -0.5244$, giving $x_{0.30} = 72 + 8(-0.5244) \approx 67.8$. Students scoring below $67.8$ fall in the bottom 30\%.

Connection to Interquartile Range

The most immediate application is the interquartile range under normality. The IQR is defined as $Q_3 - Q_1$, where $Q_1 = x_{0.25}$ and $Q_3 = x_{0.75}$. Applying the percentile formula to each quartile:

$$\mathrm{IQR} = \bigl(\mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\bigr) - \bigl(\mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.25)\bigr) = \sigma\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(0.75) - \Phi^{-1}(0.25)\bigr).$$

Since $\Phi^{-1}(0.75) \approx 0.6745$ and $\Phi^{-1}(0.25) \approx -0.6745$, we obtain the celebrated result $\mathrm{IQR} \approx 1.3490\,\sigma$. This shows that for any normal population the IQR is a direct, robust estimator of the spread $\sigma$, immune to the influence of extreme outliers that inflate the sample standard deviation.

Key theorem. If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ then $\mathrm{IQR}(X) = 2\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\cdot\sigma \approx 1.349\,\sigma$, and consequently $\hat{\sigma} = \mathrm{IQR}/1.349$ is a consistent, breakdown-point-$0.25$ estimator of $\sigma$.

# Numerical verification via SymPy
from sympy.stats import Normal, cdf, quantile
X = Normal('X', 0, 1)          # standard normal
Q1 = quantile(X)(0.25)
Q3 = quantile(X)(0.75)
iqr = N(Q3 - Q1, 6)
print(f"IQR of N(0,1) ≈ {iqr}")   # expect ≈ 1.34898

Invitation

The $\mathrm{IQR}$–$\sigma$ bridge is only the first door the inverse normal opens. The same machinery underlies reference-interval construction in clinical diagnostics, value-at-risk calculation in finance, and quantile–quantile plots used to test normality assumptions. If you wish to see how a single formula transforms medical laboratory practice—determining, for instance, which blood-glucose readings are pathological rather than merely uncommon—turn to the next chapter, where the interquartile range under normality becomes the scaffolding for evidence-based reference limits.
