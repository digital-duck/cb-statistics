Concept Book: Interquartile Range Normal

Introductory Statistics Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this chapter, we have built the machinery of the normal distribution layer by layer: its density, its cumulative distribution function, its quantile function, and the algebraic relationship between location, scale, and probability. The concept of the interquartile range of the normal distribution draws every one of those threads together into a single, computable constant that carries profound practical weight.

Definition. Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The interquartile range of $X$ is

$$\mathrm{IQR}(X) = Q_3 - Q_1 = F^{-1}(0.75) - F^{-1}(0.25),$$

where $F^{-1}$ denotes the quantile function of $X$. Because $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, the equivariance of quantiles under affine transformation gives

$$\mathrm{IQR}(X) = \sigma\bigl[\Phi^{-1}(0.75) - \Phi^{-1}(0.25)\bigr] = 2\sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75).$$

Numerically, $\Phi^{-1}(0.75) \approx 0.6745$, so

$$\boxed{\mathrm{IQR}(X) \approx 1.3490\,\sigma.}$$

Worked example. Adult human heights are well modelled by $\mathcal{N}(170\text{ cm},\,7^2\text{ cm}^2)$. The predicted interquartile range of heights in this population is $1.3490 \times 7 \approx 9.44\text{ cm}$. A box-and-whisker plot drawn from a large sample should exhibit a box of approximately this width; any dramatic departure signals model misspecification or data contamination.

Key theorem (Normal IQR–$\sigma$ bridge). For $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, the estimator

$$\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{IQR}} = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{IQR}}}{1.3490}$$

is a robust, consistent estimator of $\sigma$. Unlike the sample standard deviation $s$, which squares each deviation and therefore amplifies the influence of outliers quadratically, $\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{IQR}}$ depends only on the central half of the data. Its breakdown point — the fraction of the sample that must be corrupted before the estimate diverges — is $25\%$, compared with $0\%$ for $s$. The price paid is a modest efficiency loss: under perfect normality, $\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{IQR}}$ has asymptotic relative efficiency of approximately $0.37$ relative to $s$, but it more than recovers this loss whenever even a small fraction of observations are heavy-tailed or erroneous.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, stats, N, Rational
from sympy.stats import Normal, quantile

mu, sigma = 170, 7
X = Normal('X', mu, sigma)

Q1 = quantile(X)(Rational(1, 4))
Q3 = quantile(X)(Rational(3, 4))
iqr = N(Q3 - Q1, 6)
sigma_hat = N(iqr / 1.3490, 6)

print(f"IQR  = {iqr} cm")
print(f"σ̂   = {sigma_hat} cm   (true σ = {sigma})")

The result $\mathrm{IQR} \approx 9.443$ and $\hat\sigma \approx 7.000$ confirm the bridge formula to full precision.

This single constant — $1.3490$ — is the reason the interquartile range is the natural endpoint of our study: it transforms a geometric summary of a box plot into a calibrated estimate of the spread of an entire Gaussian population. Every application that involves detecting anomalies in sensor data, auditing clinical trial measurements, or scaling a machine-learning feature vector ultimately rests on this relationship. To see how far that reach extends, turn to the next chapter, where we apply $\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{IQR}}$ to construct a fully worked outlier-detection pipeline on a real-world dataset — and watch the robustness advantage appear in stark numerical relief.
