Concept Book: Percentile And Invnorm

Introductory Statistics Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this text, the normal distribution has served as a precise model of uncertainty: a bell-shaped probability density whose behavior is entirely governed by a mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Every technique developed along the way — standardization, the empirical rule, cumulative probability — has been building toward a single, indispensable capability: the ability to invert the distribution. The concept of percentile and inverse normal completes this arc.

Definition. Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The $p$-th percentile of $X$ is the value $x_p$ such that $P(X \leq x_p) = p$, where $0 < p < 1$. Because the cumulative distribution function $\Phi$ of the standard normal is strictly increasing, its inverse $\Phi^{-1}$ exists everywhere on $(0,1)$, and the percentile is given by

$$x_p = \mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p).$$

The function $\Phi^{-1}$ is called the inverse normal or probit function. It accepts a probability and returns the corresponding $z$-score.

Worked Example. Suppose scores on a standardized examination follow $\mathcal{N}(500, 100^2)$. A university requires applicants to score at or above the 85th percentile. We compute the cut-off score directly: $\Phi^{-1}(0.85) \approx 1.036$, so

$$x_{0.85} = 500 + 100 \times 1.036 = 603.6.$$

Any applicant scoring at least $604$ clears the threshold. No table interpolation, no trial-and-error — the inverse normal produces the answer in a single step.

Key Theorem (Quantile Equivariance). If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $Y = aX + b$ with $a > 0$, then the $p$-th percentile of $Y$ is $a\,x_p + b$. Percentiles transform linearly under affine maps, making the inverse normal stable across unit changes and rescalings.

Lab Cell.

from scipy.stats import norm
import sympy as sp

mu, sigma, p = 500, 100, 0.85
cutoff = norm.ppf(p, loc=mu, scale=sigma)
print(f"85th percentile: {cutoff:.2f}")   # 603.64

# Symbolic form
p_sym = sp.Rational(85, 100)
z = sp.erfinv(2*p_sym - 1) * sp.sqrt(2)
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(z))

Connection to Applications. The inverse normal is the engine behind every threshold-based inference. For interquartile_range_normal, it delivers the result immediately: the first and third quartiles of $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ are $x_{0.25} = \mu - 0.6745\sigma$ and $x_{0.75} = \mu + 0.6745\sigma$, so the interquartile range collapses to the closed form $\mathrm{IQR} = 1.349\sigma$. This single formula connects a resistant measure of spread — one immune to outliers — directly to the population standard deviation, giving practitioners a robust estimator without sacrificing the elegance of the Gaussian model.
