Concept Book: Test Single Mean Unknown Sd

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the test delivers. Throughout this book you have built, piece by piece, the machinery needed to reason rigorously about a population from a finite sample. You modelled randomness with probability distributions, estimated unknown parameters with confidence intervals, and learned to frame scientific questions as hypotheses. The concept test_single_mean_unknown_sd — the one-sample Student's $t$-test — is where all of that machinery locks together into a single, deployable decision procedure. Given a random sample $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ drawn from a normal population with unknown variance, it lets you test whether the true population mean $\mu$ equals a specified value $\mu_0$ without requiring any external knowledge of $\sigma$. The test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom under $H_0$, and the entire critical-region calculation flows from that single distributional fact. The replacement of $\sigma$ by the sample standard deviation $s$ is precisely the step that makes the procedure practical: in real investigations $\sigma$ is almost never known, so a test that demands it is a test that almost never applies.

A worked example. A nutritionist claims that a diet programme reduces resting heart rate to $\mu_0 = 70$ beats per minute. Twelve participants complete the programme; their mean resting rate is $\bar{x} = 66.8$ bpm with $s = 5.4$ bpm. The test statistic is

$$t = \frac{66.8 - 70}{5.4 / \sqrt{12}} = \frac{-3.2}{1.559} \approx -2.053.$$

At significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ with $\nu = 11$ degrees of freedom, the two-tailed critical value is $t_{0.025,\,11} \approx 2.201$. Because $|{-2.053}| < 2.201$, we fail to reject $H_0$; the data do not provide sufficient evidence, at this sample size, to conclude the true mean differs from 70.

Key theorem. If $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ unknown, then $T = (\bar{X} - \mu)/(S/\sqrt{n}) \sim t_{n-1}$, where $S^2$ is the unbiased sample variance. This result, due to William Gosset (1908), is exact for normal populations and robust by the Central Limit Theorem for moderately large $n$ in non-normal settings.

Lab cell.

from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, Lt
import sympy as sp

nu = 11          # degrees of freedom
t_obs = sp.Rational(-2053, 1000)   # ≈ −2.053
T = StudentT('T', nu)

p_value = 2 * float(P(T < t_obs))
print(f"Two-tailed p-value ≈ {p_value:.4f}")

Invitation. The one-sample $t$-test is not an end in itself — it is a key that opens an entire family of inferential doors. Pick any domain that matters to you: quality control on a production line, the efficacy of a clinical intervention, the calibration of a physical instrument, or the fairness of a grading rubric. In every case the question reduces, at its core, to whether a measured mean is consistent with a claimed standard. Choose one such application and follow it all the way through: collect or simulate data, state hypotheses, compute the test statistic, interpret the $p$-value, and ask what a larger sample would change. That investigation is where the mathematics stops being abstract and starts being yours.
