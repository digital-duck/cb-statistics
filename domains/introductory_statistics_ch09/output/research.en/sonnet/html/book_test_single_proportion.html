Concept Book: Test Single Proportion

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the test achieves. Every preceding chapter has built toward a single question: given a count of successes in a fixed number of trials, can we decide — with quantified risk — whether the underlying probability of success equals a claimed value? The one-sample proportion test answers exactly this question. Formally, let $X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)$ and suppose we wish to test

$$H_0 : p = p_0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_a : p \neq p_0$$

at significance level $\alpha$. The test statistic

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}, \qquad \hat{p} = \frac{X}{n},$$

converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ under $H_0$ whenever $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) \geq 10$ (the normal-approximation condition). We reject $H_0$ when $|z| > z_{\alpha/2}$, where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the upper $\alpha/2$ quantile of the standard normal. The $p$-value is $2\Phi(-|z|)$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF. This is precisely what test_single_proportion computes.

Worked example. A manufacturer claims that $p_0 = 0.05$ of its circuit boards are defective. A quality-control engineer inspects $n = 200$ boards and finds $X = 16$ defective. Then $\hat{p} = 0.08$ and

$$z = \frac{0.08 - 0.05}{\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95 / 200}} = \frac{0.03}{0.01541} \approx 1.947.$$

With $\alpha = 0.05$ and $z_{0.025} = 1.960$, we obtain $|z| = 1.947 < 1.960$, so we fail to reject $H_0$ — the observed defect rate is elevated but not yet statistically distinguishable from the claimed 5 % at this sample size.

Key theorem (Berry–Esseen). The normal approximation error is bounded:

$$\sup_x \left| P(z \leq x) - \Phi(x) \right| \leq \frac{C \rho}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}},$$

where $\rho = E|X_i - p_0|^3$ and $C < 0.4748$. This bound guarantees that the approximation tightens at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$, justifying use of the $z$-statistic for moderate $n$.

Lab cell.

from sympy.stats import Normal, cdf
from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N

p0, p_hat, n = Rational(5,100), Rational(8,100), 200
z_stat = (p_hat - p0) / sqrt(p0*(1-p0)/n)
Z = Normal('Z', 0, 1)
p_value = 2 * (1 - cdf(Z)(z_stat))
print(f"z = {N(z_stat, 4)},  p-value = {N(p_value, 4)}")

Connections to application domains. The machinery developed here is not confined to factory floors. In clinical medicine, one asks whether the response rate of a new therapy exceeds a historical benchmark — the same $z$-statistic governs the decision. In electoral polling, the fraction of respondents supporting a candidate is tested against a 50 % threshold to call a race. In A/B testing for digital products, click-through rates are proportions, and every "significant lift" claim rests on this same foundation. In epidemiology, the attack rate of a disease in an exposed cohort is compared against a baseline prevalence. Each domain substitutes its own $n$, $X$, and $p_0$, but the inferential engine is identical.

Invitation. Of these domains, the clinical-trial setting is perhaps the richest: sample-size determination, one-sided alternatives, and the ethics of stopping rules all emerge naturally from the single-proportion framework.
