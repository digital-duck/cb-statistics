Concept Book: Test Single Proportion

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this chapter we have assembled the foundational machinery of probability: the Bernoulli trial, the binomial distribution, the normal approximation via the Central Limit Theorem, and the logic of hypothesis testing. The concept of testing a single proportion is the natural endpoint because it is the first complete inferential procedure — one that begins with raw data, passes through a probabilistic model, and delivers a decision with a quantified risk of error.

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent Bernoulli($p$) random variables, where $p$ is unknown. Given a null hypothesis $H_0: p = p_0$, the one-sample $z$-test for a proportion uses the test statistic

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}},$$

where $\hat{p} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is the sample proportion. Under $H_0$, and provided $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) \geq 10$, the statistic $z$ is approximately standard normal. We reject $H_0$ at significance level $\alpha$ when $|z| > z_{\alpha/2}$ for a two-sided test.

Worked example. A factory claims that its defect rate is $p_0 = 0.05$. A quality inspector examines $n = 200$ items and finds $\hat{p} = 0.09$. Then

$$z = \frac{0.09 - 0.05}{\sqrt{0.05 \cdot 0.95 / 200}} = \frac{0.04}{0.01541} \approx 2.60.$$

At $\alpha = 0.05$, the critical value is $z_{0.025} = 1.96$. Since $2.60 > 1.96$, the inspector rejects the manufacturer's claim. The $p$-value is $2(1 - \Phi(2.60)) \approx 0.0093$, meaning that results this extreme would arise by chance fewer than one time in a hundred under $H_0$.

Key theorem (consistency). As $n \to \infty$, the power of the $z$-test against any fixed alternative $p \neq p_0$ approaches 1. That is, with enough data the test will always detect a true deviation from $p_0$, no matter how small.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, sqrt, N
from scipy import stats

p0, p_hat, n = 0.05, 0.09, 200
z = (p_hat - p0) / (p0 * (1 - p0) / n) ** 0.5
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))
print(f"z = {z:.4f}, p-value = {p_value:.4f}")

With this single procedure in hand, every domain that produces a yes/no measurement becomes tractable. A clinical trialist asks whether a new vaccine reduces infection below the historical baseline rate; a political scientist tests whether a candidate's poll share has crossed the majority threshold; a geneticist checks whether an allele frequency in a population conforms to Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. In each case the same formula absorbs the domain-specific numbers and returns a $z$-score and a $p$-value — a lingua franca for uncertainty.

Recast the vaccine example as a formal hypothesis test, compute the minimum sample size required to achieve 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$, and interpret the result in the language of public-health decision-making.
