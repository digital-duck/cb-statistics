Sampling Distribution Selection

Definition. When a statistician computes a test statistic from sample data, the validity of every subsequent inference hinges on identifying the correct sampling distribution — the probability distribution that the test statistic follows under the null hypothesis. A sampling distribution is not the distribution of the raw data; it is the distribution of a summary quantity (mean, variance, proportion, etc.) computed repeatedly across all possible samples of size $n$ drawn from a population.

This section focuses on the transition from the standard normal $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ — assumed already familiar — to a new primitive required whenever the population variance is unknown. That primitive is Student's $t$-distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, written $t_\nu$. Its density has heavier tails than $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, reflecting the extra uncertainty introduced by estimating $\sigma^2$ from the data. As $\nu \to \infty$, the $t_\nu$ density converges pointwise to the standard normal, so $Z$ is the limiting case, not a separate rule.

Selection rule. Given a single sample of size $n$ from a normal population, select $t_\nu$ with $\nu = n - 1$ whenever $\sigma^2$ is unknown and must be estimated by the sample variance $s^2$. Select $Z$ only when $\sigma^2$ is known. For large $n$, the distinction becomes negligible in practice, but using $t$ remains correct in all cases.

Worked Example. A quality engineer records the breaking strengths (in MPa) of $n = 16$ steel rods: $\bar{x} = 412.3$, $s = 8.6$. The population variance is unknown. The appropriate test statistic for $H_0: \mu = 410$ is

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{412.3 - 410}{8.6/\sqrt{16}} = \frac{2.3}{2.15} \approx 1.07,$$

which follows a $t_{15}$ distribution under $H_0$. Using $Z$ here would understate the tail probability, because $s$ carries sampling variability that $\sigma$ does not.

Theorem (Convergence of $t$ to Normal). If $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $S^2$ denotes the sample variance, then

$$\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}.$$

As $n \to \infty$, $t_{n-1} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, justifying the $Z$ approximation for large samples while confirming that $t$ is always the theoretically exact choice when $\sigma^2$ is unknown.

Lab Cell.

from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, Gt

n, s_val, xbar, mu0 = 16, 8.6, 412.3, 410
t_stat = (xbar - mu0) / (s_val / n**0.5)

T = StudentT('T', n - 1)                  # t_{15} distribution
p_value = float(2 * P(Gt(T, t_stat)))     # two-tailed p-value
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f},  p-value ≈ {p_value:.4f}")

Running this cell confirms $t \approx 1.0698$ and $p \approx 0.30$, so we fail to reject $H_0$ at any conventional significance level.
