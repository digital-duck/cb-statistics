Concept Book: Test Single Mean Unknown Sd

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the Test Achieves

The one-sample $t$-test for an unknown population standard deviation is the instrument that transforms a single batch of measurements into a defensible claim about the world. Formally, given a random sample $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ drawn from a population with unknown mean $\mu$ and unknown standard deviation $\sigma$, the test statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}$$

follows a Student's $t$-distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis $H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$. Here $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean and $s$ is the sample standard deviation. What makes this result the natural endpoint of the preceding chapters is the substitution of $s$ for $\sigma$: because $\sigma$ is almost never known in practice, replacing it with its estimate introduces extra uncertainty, and the $t$-distribution — with its heavier tails relative to the normal — accounts for that uncertainty exactly. As $n \to \infty$, $s \to \sigma$ and the $t$-distribution converges to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, recovering the $z$-test as a special case.

Worked Example

A food-safety inspector records the salt content (g per 100 g) of $n = 12$ samples from a production line: the sample yields $\bar{x} = 1.43$ g and $s = 0.18$ g. The regulatory limit is $\mu_0 = 1.50$ g. Testing $H_0 : \mu = 1.50$ against $H_1 : \mu < 1.50$ at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$:

$$t = \frac{1.43 - 1.50}{0.18 / \sqrt{12}} = \frac{-0.07}{0.0520} \approx -1.346.$$

The critical value for a one-tailed $t$-test with $\nu = 11$ degrees of freedom is $t_{0.05, 11} \approx -1.796$. Since $-1.346 > -1.796$, we fail to reject $H_0$: the evidence does not establish that mean salt content is below the regulatory threshold.

Key Theorem

Gosset's Theorem. Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ unknown. Then

$$T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1},$$

where $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2$. The proof follows from the independence of $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ (a consequence of normality) and the fact that $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, so $T$ is the ratio of a standard normal to the square root of a scaled chi-squared — the defining construction of the $t$-distribution.

Lab Cell

from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, density
from sympy import symbols, N, simplify

nu = 11          # degrees of freedom
t_obs = -1.346   # observed statistic

T = StudentT('T', nu)
p_value = float(N(P(T < t_obs)))
print(f"p-value (one-tailed) = {p_value:.4f}")
# Expected output: p-value (one-tailed) = 0.1027

Running this cell confirms that $p \approx 0.103 > 0.05$, consistent with the manual calculation: a single batch of twelve measurements is insufficient to overturn the null. With this machinery in hand — estimating, testing, and computing $p$-values entirely from sample data — you are equipped to analyse any domain where a measured quantity must be compared against a standard: clinical dosage compliance, semiconductor yield targets, environmental contamination limits, or the calibration of a scientific instrument. Choose one of these arenas and ask: what would it cost to be wrong? That question, answered rigorously through the $t$-test, is where statistics becomes consequential.
