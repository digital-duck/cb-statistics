Concept Book: Test Single Proportion

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the test accomplishes. Every concept developed in this book — Bernoulli trials, the binomial distribution, the normal approximation via the Central Limit Theorem, standard error, and the $z$-statistic — converges at a single inferential act: deciding whether an observed sample proportion $\hat{p}$ is consistent with a claimed population proportion $p_0$. The one-proportion $z$-test formalises that decision. Given $n$ independent Bernoulli trials each succeeding with unknown probability $p$, the test statistic

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}$$

is approximately $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ under the null hypothesis $H_0: p = p_0$, provided $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) \geq 10$. This approximation is not an assumption imposed from outside; it is the conclusion forced on us by the Central Limit Theorem applied to a sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$ random variables. The test is therefore the natural endpoint: it is the moment at which the probability machinery built throughout the course produces a single number whose tail area, the $p$-value, quantifies evidence against the null.

A worked example. A manufacturer claims its process yields defective units at rate $p_0 = 0.04$. An auditor inspects $n = 200$ items and finds $\hat{p} = 11/200 = 0.055$. The test statistic is

$$z = \frac{0.055 - 0.04}{\sqrt{0.04 \times 0.96 / 200}} = \frac{0.015}{0.01386} \approx 1.082.$$

For a two-sided alternative the $p$-value is $2\Phi(-|z|) \approx 0.279$, far above any conventional significance level. The auditor cannot reject the manufacturer's claim on this evidence.

The central theorem. Under $H_0: p = p_0$ and the regularity conditions above, the statistic $z$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$. The power of the test against a specific alternative $p_1 \neq p_0$ is $1 - \Phi\!\left(z_{\alpha/2} - \delta\sqrt{n}/\sigma_1\right) + \Phi\!\left(-z_{\alpha/2} - \delta\sqrt{n}/\sigma_1\right)$, where $\delta = p_1 - p_0$ and $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)}$. Power therefore increases with sample size at rate $\sqrt{n}$, a fact with direct consequences for study design.

Lab cell.

from scipy import stats

p0, p_hat, n = 0.04, 0.055, 200
z_stat, p_val = stats.proportions_ztest(
    count=int(p_hat * n), nobs=n, value=p0, alternative="two-sided"
)
print(f"z = {z_stat:.4f},  p-value = {p_val:.4f}")

The entirety of the preceding chapters — probability spaces, expectation, variance, the CLT — exists to justify that single call. You are now equipped to carry this tool into any domain where a binary outcome is observed at scale. Choose one application that matters to you and derive the sample size you would need to detect a practically meaningful departure from the null: that calculation is where inference becomes design, and design becomes consequence.
