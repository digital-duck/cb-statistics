Concept Book: Test Single Mean Unknown Sd

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every inferential concept introduced in this book — population parameters, sampling distributions, the standard error, and hypothesis testing — converges at a single, practically indispensable procedure: the one-sample $t$-test with unknown population standard deviation. This test is the natural endpoint of the development because it removes the last unrealistic assumption. The $z$-test requires $\sigma$ to be known; in practice, it never is. By estimating $\sigma$ from the sample itself, the $t$-test operates under conditions that actually arise in a laboratory, a clinic, or a factory floor.

What the test achieves. Given a random sample $X_1, \dots, X_n$ drawn from a population with unknown mean $\mu$ and unknown standard deviation $\sigma$, the procedure tests $H_0\colon \mu = \mu_0$ against a one- or two-sided alternative. The sample standard deviation $s = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2}$ replaces $\sigma$, and the test statistic

$$T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. The loss of one degree of freedom is the precise cost of estimating $\sigma$; the heavier tails of $t_{n-1}$ relative to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ encode the additional uncertainty honestly.

Worked example. A pharmaceutical laboratory claims that a tablet formulation delivers a mean active dose of $\mu_0 = 250\,\mathrm{mg}$. A quality-assurance analyst measures $n = 12$ randomly selected tablets and records $\bar{x} = 246.3\,\mathrm{mg}$ and $s = 5.8\,\mathrm{mg}$. The test statistic is

$$t = \frac{246.3 - 250}{5.8/\sqrt{12}} = \frac{-3.7}{1.674} \approx -2.21.$$

Under $H_0$ the statistic follows $t_{11}$. The two-tailed $p$-value is $2\,P(T_{11} \leq -2.21) \approx 0.049$, which falls below the conventional $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold. The analyst rejects the null hypothesis and flags the batch for re-examination.

Key theorem. If $X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ unknown, then $T = (\bar{X} - \mu_0)/(S/\sqrt{n}) \sim t_{n-1}$ exactly. This result, due to William Sealy Gosset (1908), follows from the independence of $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ and the chi-squared distribution of $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$. For non-normal populations, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees approximate validity when $n \gtrsim 30$.

Lab cell.

from scipy import stats

x_bar, mu_0, s, n = 246.3, 250, 5.8, 12
t_stat = (x_bar - mu_0) / (s / n**0.5)
p_value = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t_stat), df=n - 1)
ci_low, ci_high = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=n-1, loc=x_bar, scale=s/n**0.5)

print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"95 % CI for μ: ({ci_low:.2f}, {ci_high:.2f})")

With test_single_mean_unknown_sd mastered, every domain that reduces a question to "does the mean equal a target value?" becomes tractable: manufacturing quality control, clinical dose verification, educational assessment benchmarking, environmental compliance monitoring, and materials science. The same five-line procedure underpins them all, differing only in what $\mu_0$ represents physically.
