Concept Book: Test Single Mean Known Sd

Introductory Statistics Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the test achieves. Throughout this chapter we built, piece by piece, the machinery of inference: the sampling distribution of the mean, the standard normal as its limiting shape, and the logic of the null hypothesis. The one-sample $z$-test for a population mean with known standard deviation,

$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}},$$

is the first complete synthesis of that machinery. Given a random sample of size $n$ drawn from a population with known standard deviation $\sigma$, the statistic $z$ measures — in units of standard error — how far the observed sample mean $\bar{x}$ lies from the hypothesised population mean $\mu_0$. Under $H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$ the statistic follows exactly $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, so a $p$-value is a simple tail-area look-up. This is not an approximation: it is an exact probability statement, valid whenever the population is normal or $n$ is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to apply.

Worked example. A pharmaceutical batch is labelled $\mu_0 = 500\,\text{mg}$ with a known process standard deviation $\sigma = 8\,\text{mg}$. Quality control draws $n = 25$ tablets and records $\bar{x} = 496.4\,\text{mg}$. The test statistic is

$$z = \frac{496.4 - 500}{8/\sqrt{25}} = \frac{-3.6}{1.6} = -2.25.$$

For a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ the critical values are $\pm 1.96$. Since $|{-2.25}| > 1.96$, the batch mean is statistically discrepant from the label claim at the 5 % level, and the $p$-value $= 2\Phi(-2.25) \approx 0.024$ quantifies the strength of that evidence.

Key theorem (Neyman–Pearson). Among all tests of $H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_1 : \mu \neq \mu_0$ at significance level $\alpha$, the $z$-test is uniformly most powerful in the class of unbiased tests. Concretely, no competing procedure of the same size can achieve higher power $1 - \beta$ uniformly across all $\mu \neq \mu_0$.

Lab cell.

from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

# Parameters
x_bar, mu_0, sigma, n = 496.4, 500, 8, 25

z_stat = (x_bar - mu_0) / (sigma / n**0.5)
p_value = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z_stat))

print(f"z = {z_stat:.4f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")

# Symbolic critical-value derivation
z = sp.Symbol('z')
alpha = sp.Rational(5, 100)
cv = sp.solve(1 - sp.erf(z / sp.sqrt(2)) - alpha, z)
sp.pprint(cv)

Invitation. The pharmaceutical example above is only one face of this test. The same statistic governs questions as varied as: does a new curriculum raise mean exam scores beyond the historical norm? does a sensor network report air-quality readings consistent with the reference standard? does a population sub-group exhibit a mean biomarker level that differs from the clinical threshold? Choose the domain that matters most to you, collect your data, and let $z$ tell you what the evidence says. The next chapter takes this foundation and relaxes the assumption of known $\sigma$ — opening the door to Student's $t$-test and the full landscape of practical inference.
