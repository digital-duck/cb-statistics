What the Concept Achieves

Throughout this chapter you have built two complementary tools for bounding probability without assuming a specific distribution: Chebyshev's inequality and the empirical rule. Together they form the natural endpoint of an introductory treatment of variability, because they answer the single most important practical question a data analyst faces—how much of a population lies within a given distance of its center?—at two different levels of generality.

Chebyshev's inequality states that for any random variable $X$ with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and for any $k > 1$,

$$P\!\left(\,|X - \mu| \geq k\sigma\right) \leq \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

This bound requires nothing beyond the existence of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$; it holds for every distribution. The cost of such universality is conservatism: at $k = 2$ the bound guarantees only that at most $25\%$ of probability mass lies beyond two standard deviations. The empirical rule, valid specifically for normal (Gaussian) distributions, sharpens this dramatically: approximately $68\%$, $95\%$, and $99.7\%$ of observations fall within $1\sigma$, $2\sigma$, and $3\sigma$ of the mean, respectively. Formally, if $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$,

$$P(\mu - k\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + k\sigma) = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}\right),$$

where $\operatorname{erf}$ denotes the Gauss error function.

Worked Example

Suppose examination scores at a university are approximately normal with $\mu = 72$ and $\sigma = 8$. The empirical rule immediately tells us that roughly $95\%$ of students scored between $72 - 16 = 56$ and $72 + 16 = 88$, while Chebyshev's bound (applied to the same $k = 2$) guarantees only that at least $75\%$ did. If instead the score distribution is unknown, Chebyshev's bound is the only rigorous statement available: at least $1 - 1/4 = 75\%$ of scores lie in that interval.

Key Theorem

Chebyshev–Bienaymé. For any distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the fraction of values falling outside the interval $(\mu - k\sigma,\, \mu + k\sigma)$ cannot exceed $1/k^2$. Equivalently, at least $1 - 1/k^2$ of all probability mass is concentrated within $k$ standard deviations of the mean.

Proof sketch. Apply Markov's inequality to the non-negative random variable $(X - \mu)^2$ with threshold $(k\sigma)^2$:

$$P\!\left((X-\mu)^2 \geq k^2\sigma^2\right) \leq \frac{E[(X-\mu)^2]}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{k^2}. \qed$$

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy import symbols, Rational, erf, sqrt, N, pi

k = symbols('k', positive=True)

# Chebyshev upper bound
chebyshev_bound = Rational(1) / k**2
print("Chebyshev upper bound at k=2:", chebyshev_bound.subs(k, 2))

# Empirical rule via error function (normal distribution)
coverage = erf(k / sqrt(2))
for k_val in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(f"Normal coverage at k={k_val}: {N(coverage.subs(k, k_val), 5)}")

Running this cell produces Chebyshev's $0.25$ alongside the normal coverages $0.6827$, $0.9545$, and $0.9974$—making the gap between distribution-free pessimism and Gaussian precision vivid in a single output.

Invitation

These two rules are not mere theoretical curiosities; they are the engine behind quality-control charts, confidence-interval heuristics, anomaly detection thresholds, and the design of grading curves. If you wish to see the full power of this machinery at work, turn to the chapter on statistical process control, where Chebyshev's inequality sets conservative alarm limits for any production process and the empirical rule refines them the moment normality can be justified—transforming abstract probability bounds into decisions about whether a manufacturing line should be stopped or allowed to run.
