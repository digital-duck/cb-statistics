Concept Book: Contingency Table

Introductory Statistics Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A contingency table is the algebraic crystallization of joint categorical observation. Given two categorical random variables $X$ taking values in $\{x_1, \dots, x_r\}$ and $Y$ taking values in $\{y_1, \dots, y_c\}$, the contingency table is the $r \times c$ matrix $N$ whose entry $n_{ij}$ records the number of sample units for which $X = x_i$ and $Y = y_j$ simultaneously. The grand total $n = \sum_{i,j} n_{ij}$ induces empirical joint probabilities $\hat{p}_{ij} = n_{ij}/n$, row marginals $\hat{p}_{i\cdot} = \sum_j \hat{p}_{ij}$, and column marginals $\hat{p}_{\cdot j} = \sum_i \hat{p}_{ij}$.

Worked example. A study records 200 patients by blood type ($A, B, O$) and disease status (present, absent). Suppose the observed counts are

$$N = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 50 \\ 20 & 40 \\ 25 & 35 \end{pmatrix},$$

with row marginals $(80, 60, 60)$ and column marginals $(75, 125)$. Under the null hypothesis of independence, the expected count in cell $(i,j)$ is $e_{ij} = n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j} / n$. For cell $(1,1)$: $e_{11} = 80 \times 75 / 200 = 30$. Computing all six expected counts and comparing them to $N$ via the Pearson statistic

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(n_{ij} - e_{ij})^2}{e_{ij}}$$

yields a test of statistical independence between blood type and disease.

Key theorem (Pearson, 1900). Under $H_0: p_{ij} = p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot j}$ for all $i,j$, the statistic $\chi^2$ converges in distribution to $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$ as $n \to \infty$, provided all $e_{ij} \geq 5$.

The contingency table is the natural endpoint of this textbook because every preceding concept — sample spaces, marginal and conditional distributions, independence, and expectation — converges here into a single computable object. It transforms the abstract notion of stochastic independence into a finite matrix of counts that any practitioner can construct, inspect, and test.

Lab cell (SymPy).

from sympy.stats import ChiSquared, cdf
from sympy import Matrix, Rational, N as Neval

observed = Matrix([[30,50],[20,40],[25,35]])
n = sum(observed)
row_m = [sum(observed.row(i)) for i in range(3)]
col_m = [sum(observed.col(j)) for j in range(2)]
expected = Matrix([[Rational(row_m[i]*col_m[j], n)
                    for j in range(2)] for i in range(3)])
chi2_stat = sum((observed[i,j]-expected[i,j])**2/expected[i,j]
                for i in range(3) for j in range(2))
print("χ² =", Neval(chi2_stat, 4))   # degrees of freedom = (3-1)(2-1) = 2

The techniques built throughout this book now place you at the threshold of categorical data analysis, survival modeling, and classifier evaluation — wherever two discrete worlds intersect and you must decide whether that intersection is meaningful or merely accidental. Choose one domain that compels you, construct its contingency table, and let the $\chi^2$ statistic speak.
