What a Contingency Table Achieves

A contingency table (also called a cross-tabulation or two-way frequency table) is the canonical instrument for recording the joint distribution of two or more categorical variables over a sample. Given $n$ observations classified simultaneously by a row variable $R$ taking values $\{r_1, \ldots, r_I\}$ and a column variable $C$ taking values $\{c_1, \ldots, c_J\}$, the table entry

$$n_{ij} = \#\{\text{observations with } R = r_i \text{ and } C = c_j\}$$

encodes every pairwise frequency in a single $I \times J$ array. Marginal totals $n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}$ and $n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}$ summarise each variable in isolation, while the interior cells capture their interaction. The central question the table is built to answer is whether $R$ and $C$ are statistically independent — that is, whether

$$P(R = r_i,\, C = c_j) = P(R = r_i)\,P(C = c_j) \quad \text{for all } i, j.$$

Under independence, the expected frequency in cell $(i,j)$ is $\hat{e}_{ij} = n_{i\cdot}\,n_{\cdot j}/n$, and departure from independence is measured by Pearson's chi-squared statistic

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J} \frac{(n_{ij} - \hat{e}_{ij})^2}{\hat{e}_{ij}},$$

which follows a $\chi^2_{(I-1)(J-1)}$ distribution asymptotically. This single quantity distils an entire matrix of counts into a decision about association — a remarkable compression.

Why It Is the Natural Endpoint

Every concept developed in the preceding chapters converges here. Combinatorial counting principles determine how many distinct $(r_i, c_j)$ categories are possible. Probability axioms justify interpreting $n_{ij}/n$ as an empirical probability. The law of large numbers guarantees that those empirical probabilities approach true joint probabilities as $n \to \infty$. Independence, defined in terms of product measures, becomes a testable null hypothesis precisely because the contingency table makes joint and marginal frequencies explicit and computable in parallel.

The table is therefore not merely a display device; it is the point at which symbolic structure (categorical classification), numerical computation (frequency counts, chi-squared arithmetic), and inferential reasoning (hypothesis testing) are unified into a single coherent object.

Worked Example

Consider a clinical trial enrolling $n = 200$ patients, classified by treatment ($T$: Drug, Placebo) and outcome ($O$: Recovered, Not recovered):

| | Recovered | Not recovered | Row total | |--------------|-----------|---------------|-----------| | Drug | 90 | 10 | 100 | | Placebo | 60 | 40 | 100 | | Column total | 150 | 50 | 200 |

Expected frequencies under independence:

$$\hat{e}_{11} = \frac{100 \times 150}{200} = 75, \quad \hat{e}_{12} = \frac{100 \times 50}{200} = 25,$$ $$\hat{e}_{21} = 75, \quad \hat{e}_{22} = 25.$$

The chi-squared statistic is

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(90-75)^2}{75} + \frac{(10-25)^2}{25} + \frac{(60-75)^2}{75} + \frac{(40-25)^2}{25} = 3 + 9 + 3 + 9 = 24.$$

With $(2-1)(2-1) = 1$ degree of freedom and $\chi^2_{0.001,1} \approx 10.8$, the result is highly significant: treatment and recovery are not independent at any conventional level.

Key Theorem

Fisher's exact test (small-sample complement). When expected cell counts are small ($\hat{e}_{ij} < 5$ for any cell), the chi-squared approximation is unreliable. In a $2 \times 2$ table with fixed marginals $(n_{1\cdot}, n_{2\cdot}, n_{\cdot 1}, n_{\cdot 2})$, the exact probability of observing a table at least as extreme as the data is

$$p = \frac{\binom{n_{1\cdot}}{n_{11}}\binom{n_{2\cdot}}{n_{21}}}{\binom{n}{n_{\cdot 1}}},$$

computed by summing over all tables with the same marginals that are equally or more extreme. This guarantees a valid $p$-value regardless of sample size, connecting the combinatorial foundations of the course directly to rigorous inference.

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy.stats import ChiSquared, P
from sympy import symbols, Rational, sqrt, N

# Observed and expected counts from the worked example
observed = [[90, 10], [60, 40]]
row_totals = [100, 100]
col_totals = [150, 50]
n = 200

chi2_stat = sum(
    (observed[i][j] - row_totals[i]*col_totals[j]/n)**2
    / (row_totals[i]*col_totals[j]/n)
    for i in range(2) for j in range(2)
)

X = ChiSquared('X', 1)          # 1 degree of freedom
p_value = float(P(X > chi2_stat))

print(f"chi² = {chi2_stat:.4f}")
print(f"p-value ≈ {p_value:.6f}")

Running this cell should return $\chi^2 = 24.0000$ and a $p$-value on the order of $10^{-6}$, confirming the hand calculation and giving direct experience of how a symbolic statistics library operationalises the theory.

Invitation

The contingency table is the gateway to an entire landscape of categorical data analysis: log-linear models generalise independence testing to multi-way tables; measures of association such as Cramér's $V$ and the odds ratio quantify the strength of dependence beyond a binary reject/fail decision; and in machine learning, the confusion matrix — a two-way table of predicted versus true labels — uses identical arithmetic to evaluate classifier performance. Choose any one of these extensions and you will find that the machinery developed in this chapter carries over almost without modification. The next chapter pursues the log-linear model in depth; readers drawn to classification are invited to begin there and return to the odds ratio once its connection to logistic regression becomes apparent.
