Concept Book: Tree Diagram

Introductory Statistics Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A tree diagram is a finite, acyclic, connected graph $T = (V, E)$ in which exactly one vertex, called the root, has in-degree zero, and every other vertex has in-degree exactly one. Equivalently, between any two vertices there exists a unique path. The depth of a vertex $v$ is the length of the unique path from the root to $v$; vertices at depth $k$ form the $k$-th level of the tree. When each non-leaf vertex has a fixed branching factor $b$, the tree is called a $b$-ary tree, and the number of leaves at depth $d$ is exactly $b^d$.

Worked example. Consider a fair coin flipped three times. Construct the complete binary tree of outcomes: the root represents the initial state, each left-child edge is labeled $H$ (probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$) and each right-child edge $T$ (probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$). At depth 3 there are $2^3 = 8$ leaves. The probability assigned to any leaf is the product of edge probabilities along the unique root-to-leaf path, giving $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \tfrac{1}{8}$ for each leaf. Summing over the three leaves containing exactly two heads,

$$P(\text{exactly two heads}) = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8},$$

which agrees with the binomial formula $\binom{3}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3$.

Key theorem (Multiplicative Path Rule). Let $T$ be a rooted tree in which each edge $(u, v)$ carries a conditional probability $p(v \mid u)$, with $\sum_{v \in \text{children}(u)} p(v \mid u) = 1$ for every internal vertex $u$. Then the probability of any leaf $\ell$ at depth $d$ is

$$P(\ell) = \prod_{i=0}^{d-1} p(v_{i+1} \mid v_i),$$

where $v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_d = \ell$ is the root-to-leaf path. Furthermore, $\sum_{\ell \in \text{leaves}} P(\ell) = 1$.

This theorem is the reason the tree diagram stands as the natural endpoint of the present chapter. Every concept encountered along the way—sample spaces, conditional probability, independence, and the chain rule—finds its clearest geometric embodiment in the tree structure. The branching encodes conditioning; the path product encodes the chain rule; independence manifests as a uniform branching factor that factorises cleanly. No other single diagram unifies these ideas with the same economy.

Tree diagrams extend far beyond elementary probability. In decision analysis, internal vertices represent choices and nature-moves, and backward induction on the tree yields optimal strategies. In computer science, parse trees decompose grammatical sentences and underlie every compiler. In evolutionary biology, phylogenetic trees encode the branching history of species. In each domain the mathematics is the same: a root, directed edges carrying weights or labels, and the Multiplicative Path Rule governing inference along paths.
