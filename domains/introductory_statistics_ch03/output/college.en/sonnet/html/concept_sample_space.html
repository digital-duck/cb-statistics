Sample Space

Definition. A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. It is conventionally denoted $\Omega$ (or $S$). Each element $\omega \in \Omega$ is called a sample point or elementary outcome. A sample space may be finite, countably infinite, or uncountably infinite, depending on the nature of the experiment.

Formally, given a random experiment $\mathcal{E}$, the sample space $\Omega$ satisfies two properties: (i) every conceivable result of $\mathcal{E}$ corresponds to exactly one element of $\Omega$, and (ii) exactly one element of $\Omega$ is realized each time $\mathcal{E}$ is performed. These two properties are sometimes stated as exhaustiveness (no outcome is missing) and mutual exclusivity (no two distinct elements can both be realized simultaneously). Specifying $\Omega$ completely and unambiguously is the first step in any probabilistic model; subsequent constructs — events, probability measures — are built on top of it.

Worked Example. Consider rolling a standard fair die once. The experiment $\mathcal{E}$ produces one face value from $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, so

$$\Omega = \{1,\, 2,\, 3,\, 4,\, 5,\, 6\}.$$

This sample space is finite with $|\Omega| = 6$. Now suppose instead that we record the exact time (in seconds) until a radioactive atom decays. Any non-negative real value is physically possible, giving the uncountable sample space $\Omega = [0, \infty)$. The two examples illustrate how the same modelling choice — what counts as one outcome — determines the entire structure of $\Omega$.

Key Theorem (Uniqueness of the Sample Space). Given a precise description of a random experiment $\mathcal{E}$, there exists a unique minimal sample space $\Omega^$ such that every other valid sample space for $\mathcal{E}$ can be obtained from $\Omega^$ by partitioning its elements. In other words, coarser descriptions of outcomes correspond to quotients of $\Omega^*$, never to genuinely different foundations. This theorem underscores why precision in defining $\Omega$ matters: a poorly specified sample space cannot be recovered by later refinement — it must be replaced entirely.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

from sympy import FiniteSet, Interval, oo

# Finite sample space: one roll of a fair six-sided die
omega_die = FiniteSet(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)
print("Sample space (die):", omega_die)
print("Cardinality:", len(omega_die))   # 6

# Uncountable sample space: radioactive decay time
omega_decay = Interval(0, oo)
print("Sample space (decay):", omega_decay)

# Verify membership for a candidate outcome
t = 2.5                                 # seconds
print(f"Is t={t} a valid outcome?", t in omega_decay)  # True

Running this cell confirms that omega_die contains exactly six elements and that any non-negative real number is a valid member of omega_decay. The contrast between FiniteSet and Interval in SymPy mirrors the mathematical distinction between finite and uncountably infinite sample spaces — a distinction that will matter when assigning probabilities in the next section.
