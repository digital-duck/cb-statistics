Concept Book: Contingency Table

Introductory Statistics Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A contingency table is the natural resting place of everything this book has built. Formally, given two categorical random variables $X$ taking values in $\{x_1,\dots,x_r\}$ and $Y$ taking values in $\{y_1,\dots,y_c\}$, a contingency table is the $r \times c$ matrix $N$ whose entry $n_{ij}$ records the observed joint frequency of the event $\{X = x_i, Y = y_j\}$ in a sample of size $n = \sum_{i,j} n_{ij}$. The marginal counts $n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}$ and $n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}$ recover the individual distributions of $X$ and $Y$ respectively.

Worked example. Suppose a clinical study records treatment type ($X \in \{\text{Drug},\text{Placebo}\}$) and outcome ($Y \in \{\text{Recovered},\text{Not recovered}\}$) for $n = 200$ patients:

$$N = \begin{pmatrix} 80 & 20 \\ 40 & 60 \end{pmatrix}$$

The marginals are $n_{1\cdot} = 100,\; n_{2\cdot} = 100,\; n_{\cdot 1} = 120,\; n_{\cdot 2} = 80$. Under the null hypothesis of independence, the expected count in cell $(i,j)$ is $\hat{e}_{ij} = n_{i\cdot}\,n_{\cdot j}/n$; here $\hat{e}_{11} = 100 \cdot 120/200 = 60$, revealing a substantial departure from what chance alone would predict.

Key theorem (Pearson, 1900). Under $H_0$ of independence and for large $n$, the statistic

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(n_{ij} - \hat{e}_{ij})^2}{\hat{e}_{ij}}$$

converges in distribution to $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$. Rejection of $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ establishes that $X$ and $Y$ are statistically associated — the indispensable first step in causal reasoning.

Lab cell.

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
import sympy as sp

N = [[80, 20], [40, 60]]
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(N, correction=False)
print(f"chi2 = {chi2:.4f}, p = {p:.4e}, dof = {dof}")
print("Expected counts:\n", expected)

The contingency table thus unifies counting, probability, and inference into a single object. It is the instrument through which raw co-occurrence data becomes a testable scientific claim — whether the question concerns treatment efficacy, linguistic co-occurrence, or demographic correlation.

Choose any one of these arenas and carry the $\chi^2$ test one step further: compute the standardised residuals $r_{ij} = (n_{ij} - \hat{e}_{ij})/\sqrt{\hat{e}_{ij}}$, map them back onto the table, and let the pattern tell you not merely that association exists, but where it lives.
