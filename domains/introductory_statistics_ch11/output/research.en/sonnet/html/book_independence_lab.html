Concept Book: Independence Lab

Introductory Statistics Ch11 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this text, each preceding concept—span, basis, dimension, and linear dependence—has served as scaffolding. The laboratory on independence is where the scaffolding comes down and the building stands on its own. Independence is not merely a negative condition ("no vector is a linear combination of the others"); it is the positive assertion that a set of vectors, functions, or random variables carries no redundant information. Every element contributes something the others cannot, and that irreducibility is precisely what makes the concept load-bearing across mathematics, statistics, and computation.

What the lab achieves. Working through independence_lab equips you to answer the foundational question of any quantitative discipline: how many genuinely distinct directions of variation exist in my data or system? Given a finite set $S = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the lab asks you to form the matrix

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_k \end{bmatrix}$$

and reduce it to row-echelon form. The number of pivot columns equals $\dim(\operatorname{span}(S))$; a set is linearly independent if and only if that count equals $k$, meaning the system $Ac = \mathbf{0}$ admits only the trivial solution $c = \mathbf{0}$.

Worked example. Let $v_1 = (1, 2, 0)^\top$, $v_2 = (0, 1, 1)^\top$, $v_3 = (1, 0, -2)^\top$. Row-reducing $A$ yields

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1,\; R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Two pivots, three vectors: the set is dependent, with $v_3 = v_1 - 2v_2$. The lab makes this certificate explicit and computable.

Key theorem. A set of $k$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is linearly independent if and only if the rank of the matrix they form equals $k$. As a corollary, no linearly independent set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can have more than $n$ elements—a result that simultaneously bounds the size of every basis and explains why $n$-dimensional space requires exactly $n$ coordinates to describe a point without ambiguity.

Lab cell.

from sympy import Matrix

A = Matrix([[1, 0, 1],
            [2, 1, 0],
            [0, 1, -2]])

rref, pivots = A.rref()
print("RREF:\n", rref)
print("Pivot columns:", pivots)
print("Linearly independent?", len(pivots) == A.cols)

Running this cell surfaces the pivot structure directly, turning an abstract criterion into a three-line verdict.

Why this is the natural endpoint. Every major theme of the book converges here. Spanning sets ensure coverage; independence ensures efficiency; together they define a basis, and a basis defines a coordinate system. Without independence, coordinates are ambiguous—two descriptions of the same point. With it, every point has a unique address, and computation becomes trustworthy.

Of all the directions opened by this concept, one stands out as immediately actionable: principal component analysis, where identifying the largest independent directions of variance in a data matrix is precisely the task of finding a basis adapted to the data's geometry. If you wish to see how the row-reduction algorithm of this lab scales to datasets with thousands of features and yields the compressed representations behind modern image and language models, turn to Chapter 9, where independence meets the singular value decomposition and the theory becomes engineering.
