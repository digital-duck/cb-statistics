Concept Book: Goodness Of Fit Lab

Introductory Statistics Ch11 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this text, we have built the machinery of statistical inference one layer at a time: probability distributions, estimators, sampling distributions, and hypothesis tests. The goodness-of-fit laboratory is the natural capstone because it asks the most fundamental question one can pose about a dataset: does reality match the model we have assumed? Every earlier technique took a distributional family for granted; goodness-of-fit testing is the moment we subject that assumption to scrutiny.

What the laboratory achieves. Given an observed sample $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ and a hypothesised distribution $F_0$, a goodness-of-fit test produces a test statistic that measures the discrepancy between the empirical distribution $\hat{F}_n$ and $F_0$. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic,

$$D_n = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \bigl|\hat{F}_n(x) - F_0(x)\bigr|,$$

is perhaps the cleanest example: it is the largest vertical gap between the empirical and theoretical CDFs. Under the null hypothesis $H_0: F = F_0$, the limiting distribution of $\sqrt{n}\,D_n$ is the Kolmogorov distribution, giving an exact $p$-value without any binning artefacts. The chi-squared test,

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{(O_k - E_k)^2}{E_k},$$

complements this by operating on counts across $K$ cells, converging in distribution to $\chi^2_{K-1}$ when all expected counts $E_k = n p_k$ are sufficiently large. Together, the two tests cover continuous and discrete settings respectively, forming a complete diagnostic toolkit.

Worked example. Suppose $n = 200$ exam scores are hypothesised to follow $\mathcal{N}(70, 100)$. Standardise each observation, compute $\hat{F}_{200}$ at each unique value, and evaluate $D_{200}$. If $D_{200} = 0.048$ then $\sqrt{200}\cdot 0.048 \approx 0.679$; consulting the Kolmogorov table gives $p \approx 0.75$, so we do not reject the normal model.

Key theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli). For any continuous $F$,

$$D_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,$$

guaranteeing that with enough data the empirical distribution recovers the true one—making $D_n$ a consistent diagnostic regardless of the alternative.

Laboratory cell.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
data = rng.normal(loc=70, scale=10, size=200)

ks_stat, p_value = stats.kstest(data, "norm", args=(70, 10))
print(f"KS statistic = {ks_stat:.4f},  p-value = {p_value:.4f}")

chi2_stat, p_chi2 = stats.normaltest(data)
print(f"χ² omnibus  = {chi2_stat:.4f},  p-value = {p_chi2:.4f}")

Running this cell produces both a KS and an omnibus chi-squared result, letting you compare their sensitivity side by side and observe how sample size shifts the boundary between "plausible" and "implausible" models.

The tools assembled here open every domain in which a distributional assumption underlies a decision. To deepen your understanding, choose one application that matters to you—quality control, survival analysis, or financial returns—and carry a real dataset through the full pipeline: fit the parameters, run both tests, plot the Q–Q diagram, and report what the $p$-value actually licenses you to conclude. That exercise is where statistical reasoning becomes scientific judgment.
