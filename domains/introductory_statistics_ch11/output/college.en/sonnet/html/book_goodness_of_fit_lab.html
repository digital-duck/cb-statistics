Concept Book: Goodness Of Fit Lab

Payoff

Throughout this text we have built, piece by piece, the machinery of probability distributions, parameter estimation, and hypothesis testing. The goodness-of-fit lab is the natural endpoint of that journey: it is the moment we stop asking what does this distribution look like in theory? and start asking does this distribution describe the world I actually observe?

Definition. Let $O_1, O_2, \ldots, O_k$ be observed frequencies in $k$ mutually exclusive categories from a sample of size $n$, and let $E_i = n p_i$ be the expected frequencies under a null hypothesis $H_0$ specifying probabilities $p_1, \ldots, p_k$. The Pearson chi-squared statistic is

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i},$$

which, under $H_0$ and when every $E_i \geq 5$, follows approximately a $\chi^2$ distribution with $\nu = k - 1 - m$ degrees of freedom, where $m$ is the number of parameters estimated from the data.

Worked example. A six-sided die is rolled 120 times. Under the fair-die hypothesis each face appears with probability $\frac{1}{6}$, giving $E_i = 20$ for all $i$. Suppose the observed counts are $(18, 22, 25, 17, 20, 18)$. Then

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(18-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(22-20)^2}{20} + \cdots + \frac{(18-20)^2}{20} = \frac{4+4+25+9+0+4}{20} = 2.3.$$

With $\nu = 5$ degrees of freedom the critical value at $\alpha = 0.05$ is $\chi^2_{0.05,5} \approx 11.07$. Since $2.3 \ll 11.07$ we fail to reject $H_0$; the die is consistent with fairness.

Key theorem (Pearson, 1900). As $n \to \infty$ the statistic $\chi^2$ converges in distribution to $\chi^2(\nu)$ whenever the true cell probabilities are positive. This result requires no assumption about the shape of the underlying population—only that observations are independent and the sample is large enough to guarantee adequate expected cell counts.

Lab cell.

from scipy.stats import chisquare
import sympy as sp

observed  = [18, 22, 25, 17, 20, 18]
stat, p   = chisquare(observed)          # uniform expected by default
print(f"χ² = {stat:.4f},  p-value = {p:.4f}")

# Symbolic verification of the formula
O, E = sp.symbols('O E', positive=True)
cell_contribution = (O - E)**2 / E
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(cell_contribution))

The goodness-of-fit test is not a terminus but a gateway. It underlies model validation in every quantitative field: confirming that residuals from a linear regression follow a normal distribution, verifying that interarrival times in a queueing system are exponentially distributed, or testing whether genotype frequencies in a population obey Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Each application plugs different theoretical probabilities $p_i$ into the same $\chi^2$ skeleton you have just derived.

Choose one domain that speaks to you—genetics, queuing theory, or model diagnostics—and carry the machinery one step further. The test you can now derive and compute from first principles is the same one that appears in every published paper in that field.
