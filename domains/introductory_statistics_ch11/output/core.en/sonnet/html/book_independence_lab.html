What the Independence Lab Achieves

Statistical independence is one of the most load-bearing ideas in all of mathematics. Two events $A$ and $B$ defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ are independent if and only if

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).$$

More generally, a collection of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ is mutually independent when the joint distribution factors completely:

$$f_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f_{X_i}(x_i).$$

The independence lab synthesises every tool developed across this textbook — set-theoretic probability, conditional expectation, moment-generating functions, and symbolic computation — into a single, testable criterion. By working through the lab, a student moves from checking independence on paper to computing it: verifying marginals, comparing joint densities, and confirming or refuting the factorisation numerically and symbolically.

A Worked Verification

Let $(X, Y)$ have joint density $f(x,y) = 6x$ on the region $0 < x < 1,\; 0 < y < x$, and zero elsewhere. The marginals are

$$f_X(x) = \int_0^x 6x\,dy = 6x^2, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 6x\,dx = 3(1-y^2).$$

Because $f_X(x)\cdot f_Y(y) = 18x^2(1-y^2) \neq 6x = f(x,y)$ on the support, $X$ and $Y$ are not independent. The lab cell below reproduces this conclusion algorithmically, making the failure of factorisation visible rather than merely asserted.

The Central Theorem

Theorem (Independence and Covariance). If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$. The converse fails in general: zero covariance does not imply independence.

This asymmetry is precisely what the lab exposes. Students generate distributions with $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ that are nonetheless dependent, training the intuition that linear correlation is a strictly weaker condition than independence.

Lab Cell

from sympy import *
x, y = symbols('x y', positive=True)
f = 6*x                          # joint density on 0<y<x<1
fX = integrate(f, (y, 0, x))
fY = integrate(f, (x, y, 1))
print("Independent:", simplify(fX * fY - f) == 0)

An Invitation

Independence underlies every domain where data streams are assumed to arrive without cross-contamination — from clinical trial design, where treatment and control arms must be randomised, to machine-learning pipelines, where i.i.d. sampling guarantees convergence proofs. Choose one domain that matters to you, replicate its independence assumption in SymPy, and then deliberately break it: observe how estimation, inference, and prediction each degrade.
