Concept Book: Real World Two Group Comparisons

Introductory Statistics Ch10 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every statistical journey through probability distributions, sampling theory, and hypothesis testing ultimately arrives at the same practical question: did this intervention make a difference? The concept of real-world two-group comparison is the formal answer. It fuses the abstract machinery built across this book into a single, decision-ready procedure that quantifies the evidence for a difference between two populations and controls the probability of a wrong conclusion.

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{P}_1$ and $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{P}_2$ be independent random samples. A two-group comparison is a statistical procedure that tests $H_0: \theta_1 = \theta_2$ against $H_1: \theta_1 \neq \theta_2$ (or a one-sided variant), where $\theta_i$ is a parameter of interest—commonly the population mean $\mu_i$—using a test statistic whose null distribution is known, and that produces an effect-size estimate together with a confidence interval.

Worked Example. A researcher records systolic blood pressure (mmHg) for $n_1 = 40$ patients given a new drug and $n_2 = 42$ given a placebo. The sample means are $\bar{x} = 128.3$ and $\bar{y} = 134.7$, with standard deviations $s_1 = 11.2$ and $s_2 = 12.5$. The Welch $t$-statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \bar{y}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} = \frac{128.3 - 134.7}{\sqrt{\dfrac{125.44}{40} + \dfrac{156.25}{42}}} \approx -2.51.$$

With degrees of freedom approximated by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula ($\nu \approx 78$), the two-sided $p$-value is approximately $0.014$. At significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ the null hypothesis is rejected; the drug is associated with a mean reduction of $6.4$ mmHg ($95\%$ CI: $[1.3, 11.5]$).

Key Theorem (Welch's $t$-test validity). Under $H_0$ and the assumption of independence and approximate normality of sample means (guaranteed by the Central Limit Theorem for $n_i \gtrsim 30$), the statistic $t$ defined above follows, asymptotically, a $t$-distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom given by

$$\nu = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1} + \dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}.$$

This result is robust to unequal variances, making it the preferred choice over Student's pooled-variance $t$-test in virtually all practical settings.

Lab Cell.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
drug    = rng.normal(loc=128.3, scale=11.2, size=40)
placebo = rng.normal(loc=134.7, scale=12.5, size=42)

t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(drug, placebo, equal_var=False)
ci = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(drug)+len(placebo)-2,
                      loc=drug.mean()-placebo.mean(),
                      scale=stats.sem(drug-placebo[:40]))
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_val:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI for mean difference: ({ci[0]:.2f}, {ci[1]:.2f})")

This is the natural endpoint of the book because every earlier concept—probability spaces, estimators, the Central Limit Theorem, confidence intervals, $p$-values—is a load-bearing component of this single procedure. Clinical trials use it to license medicines; economists use it to evaluate policy interventions; engineers use it in A/B experiments to ship better products; biologists use it to distinguish treatment from control in every bench experiment published today. The two-group comparison is not merely one technique among many; it is the canonical act of statistical inference about cause and effect.
