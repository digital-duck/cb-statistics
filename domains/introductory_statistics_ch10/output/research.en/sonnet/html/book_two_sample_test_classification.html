What Two-Sample Test Classification Achieves

Throughout this chapter we have built the machinery of hypothesis testing piece by piece: null distributions, test statistics, $p$-values, and the decision boundary that separates "consistent with chance" from "statistically distinguishable." Two-sample test classification is the moment that machinery becomes a decision engine. Given two independent samples $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_m)$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$, drawn from populations with unknown means $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$, the procedure classifies the pair as either indistinguishable ($H_0\colon \mu_X = \mu_Y$) or distinguishable ($H_1\colon \mu_X \neq \mu_Y$) at a pre-specified significance level $\alpha$. The Welch $t$-statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \bar{y}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_X^2}{m} + \dfrac{s_Y^2}{n}}}$$

follows an approximate $t$-distribution with degrees of freedom given by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula, and the classification rule is: reject $H_0$ if $|t| > t_{\alpha/2,\,\nu}$. This is the natural endpoint of the chapter because it converts a continuous numerical comparison into a binary scientific verdict—precisely the output that downstream reasoning and real-world action require.

Worked Example

A nutritionist measures resting heart rate in $m = 30$ athletes and $n = 28$ sedentary adults, obtaining $\bar{x} = 58.4$, $s_X = 6.1$, $\bar{y} = 72.9$, $s_Y = 8.3$. Computing $t \approx -8.47$ against $t_{0.025,\,\nu \approx 50} \approx 2.01$, we classify the groups as distinguishable at $\alpha = 0.05$. The magnitude of $|t|$ far exceeds the critical value, so the conclusion is robust to mild departures from normality by the Central Limit Theorem.

Key Theorem (Welch, 1947)

Under $H_0$, if $m, n \to \infty$, the statistic $t$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; for finite samples the $t_\nu$ approximation controls Type I error at $\alpha$ even when $\sigma_X^2 \neq \sigma_Y^2$, making equal-variance assumptions unnecessary.

Lab Cell

from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

x = [58, 61, 55, 60, 57]   # athlete sample (abbreviated)
y = [70, 75, 68, 74, 73]   # sedentary sample (abbreviated)

t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f},  p = {p_val:.4f}")
# Classify:
alpha = 0.05
print("Distinguishable" if p_val < alpha else "Indistinguishable")

Connection to Real-World Two-Group Comparisons

This classification framework is the lingua franca of applied science. In clinical trials it determines whether a new drug produces a measurably different outcome than a placebo. In A/B testing it decides whether a redesigned web page significantly changes user conversion rates. In manufacturing quality control it flags whether two production batches meet the same specification. In each domain the underlying question is identical: do two observed samples arise from populations with the same mean, or not? Two-sample test classification answers that question with a principled, quantifiable risk of error—a guarantee no informal comparison can provide.
