Concept Book: Real World Two Group Comparisons

Introductory Statistics Ch10 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The machinery developed across this book—descriptive statistics, probability distributions, sampling theory, and inferential reasoning—converges at a single practical question: when two groups differ on a measured outcome, is that difference real or merely the noise of random sampling? The concept of real-world two-group comparisons formalises this question into a rigorous decision procedure, transforming raw data into defensible conclusions.

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n_1} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2)$ and $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{n_2} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)$ be independent samples drawn from two populations sharing an unknown common variance. The two-sample $t$-statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}}{s_p\,\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_1}+\tfrac{1}{n_2}}}, \qquad s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$.

Worked example. A clinical team records resting heart rates (bpm) for $n_1 = 15$ patients on a new medication ($\bar{X} = 68.4$, $s_1 = 5.2$) and $n_2 = 18$ controls ($\bar{Y} = 73.1$, $s_2 = 6.0$). The pooled standard deviation is $s_p \approx 5.65$, giving $t \approx -2.47$ on $\nu = 31$ degrees of freedom. Because $|t| > t_{0.025,\,31} \approx 2.04$, the team rejects $H_0$ at the 5% significance level and concludes the medication is associated with a statistically significant reduction in heart rate.

Key theorem (Welch–Satterthwaite extension). When the equal-variance assumption is untenable—a common occurrence in practice—the degrees of freedom are approximated by

$$\hat{\nu} = \frac{\left(\tfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\tfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}},$$

preserving valid Type-I error control without requiring $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$.

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

group_a = np.array([68, 70, 65, 72, 67, 69, 71, 66, 68, 70,
                    65, 73, 68, 69, 67])          # medication
group_b = np.array([74, 72, 76, 71, 75, 73, 78, 70, 74, 72,
                    76, 71, 75, 73, 70, 74, 72, 71])  # control

t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(group_a, group_b, equal_var=False)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")

The two-group comparison is not merely a statistical exercise; it is the lingua franca of evidence-based inquiry. Every domain that must choose between two conditions—two drugs, two curricula, two engineering designs—ultimately reduces its question to the framework studied here. Mastering it opens the door to the full landscape of experimental science.
