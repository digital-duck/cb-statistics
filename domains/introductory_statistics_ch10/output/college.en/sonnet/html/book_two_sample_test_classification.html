Concept Book: Two Sample Test Classification

Introductory Statistics Ch10 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The entire architecture of this chapter — random variables, sampling distributions, the central limit theorem, hypothesis testing, and the $t$-statistic — was constructed in service of a single, powerful operation: two-sample test classification. Given two independent samples $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$, drawn from populations with unknown means $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$, the procedure classifies the observed difference $\bar{x} - \bar{y}$ as either a genuine signal or plausible noise. Formally, it renders a binary verdict on the null hypothesis

$$H_0 : \mu_X = \mu_Y \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1 : \mu_X \neq \mu_Y,$$

by computing the Welch $t$-statistic

$$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \bar{y}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_X^2}{m} + \dfrac{s_Y^2}{n}}},$$

and comparing it against the $t$-distribution with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom $\nu$. If $|t| > t_{\alpha/2,\,\nu}$, the difference is classified as statistically significant; otherwise, it is not. Classification here is not metaphorical: the test partitions the space of possible outcomes into a rejection region and a retention region, making it a literal decision boundary over the real line.

Worked example. Suppose a pharmacology trial records pain scores for $m = 30$ patients receiving a new analgesic ($\bar{x} = 4.1$, $s_X = 1.2$) and $n = 28$ controls ($\bar{y} = 5.3$, $s_Y = 1.5$). Then $t \approx -3.6$ with $\nu \approx 51$. At significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, the critical value $t_{0.025,\,51} \approx 2.01$, so $|t| > t_{\alpha/2,\,\nu}$ and we classify the groups as genuinely different: the drug reduces reported pain.

Theorem (Welch, 1947). Under $H_0$ and with the Satterthwaite approximation for $\nu$, the statistic $t$ is asymptotically distributed as $t_\nu$, controlling the Type I error rate at $\alpha$ even when $\sigma_X^2 \neq \sigma_Y^2$.

This result unlocks real-world two-group comparisons across every empirical discipline. In medicine, it distinguishes treated from untreated cohorts. In economics, it separates wage distributions across demographic groups. In materials science, it classifies alloy batches by tensile strength. In education research, it evaluates whether an intervention raises test scores. In each domain, the same algebraic object — a standardized difference of means — does the classificatory work.

# Lab cell — Welch two-sample t-test in SymPy / SciPy
from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = rng.normal(loc=4.1, scale=1.2, size=30)
y = rng.normal(loc=5.3, scale=1.5, size=28)

t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(x, y, equal_var=False)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f},  p = {p_value:.4f}")
# Classify: reject H₀ if p < 0.05
print("Verdict:", "significant difference" if p_value < 0.05 else "no significant difference")
