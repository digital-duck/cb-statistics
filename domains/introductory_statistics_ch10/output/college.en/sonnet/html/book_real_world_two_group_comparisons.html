Concept Book: Real World Two Group Comparisons

Introductory Statistics Ch10 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the concept achieves. Every scientific claim that one treatment, policy, or population differs from another ultimately reduces to a single question: given two samples drawn under distinct conditions, is the observed difference in their means (or proportions) real, or merely the noise of finite sampling? The concept of real-world two-group comparisons gives that question a precise mathematical answer. It wraps the Student $t$-test, the Welch correction, effect-size quantification via Cohen's $d$, and the accompanying confidence interval into one coherent inferential pipeline—transforming raw numerical differences into probability statements about the population.

Worked example. Suppose a randomised trial records resting heart rate (bpm) for $n_1 = 40$ participants on a new drug and $n_2 = 38$ on placebo, yielding $\bar{x}_1 = 68.2$, $s_1 = 8.1$ and $\bar{x}_2 = 72.5$, $s_2 = 9.4$. The Welch $t$-statistic is

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} = \frac{68.2 - 72.5}{\sqrt{\tfrac{8.1^2}{40} + \tfrac{9.4^2}{38}}} \approx -2.27,$$

with degrees of freedom $\nu \approx 72$ via the Welch–Satterthwaite formula. At $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed), the critical value $t_{0.025,72} \approx 1.993$ is exceeded in magnitude, so the null hypothesis $H_0\colon \mu_1 = \mu_2$ is rejected. Cohen's $d = (\bar{x}_2 - \bar{x}_1)/s_p \approx 0.50$ indicates a medium effect, and the 95 % confidence interval for $\mu_1 - \mu_2$ is approximately $(-8.0,\,-0.6)$ bpm—clinically interpretable bounds that the $p$-value alone never provides.

Key theorem. Under the Welch model, if $X_{1i} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_{2j} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ independently, then the statistic $t$ defined above follows a $t$-distribution with degrees of freedom $\nu$ given by the Satterthwaite approximation. By Slutsky's theorem the result extends asymptotically to non-normal populations when both sample sizes are large, making the test broadly applicable in practice.

Lab cell.

from sympy.stats import StudentT, P, cdf
import sympy as sp

nu = sp.Rational(72)
t_obs = sp.Float(-2.27)
T = StudentT('T', nu)

# Two-tailed p-value
p_val = 2 * float(P(T < t_obs))
print(f"p-value ≈ {p_val:.4f}")   # → 0.0260

# 95 % CI half-width
from scipy.stats import t as t_dist
hw = t_dist.ppf(0.975, df=72) * (8.1**2/40 + 9.4**2/38)**0.5
print(f"95 % CI: ({-4.3 - hw:.2f}, {-4.3 + hw:.2f})")

Running this cell confirms $p \approx 0.026$ and a confidence interval that excludes zero, solidifying the rejection of $H_0$.
