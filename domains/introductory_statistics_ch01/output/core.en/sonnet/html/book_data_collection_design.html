Concept Book: Data Collection Design

Introductory Statistics Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What data collection design achieves. Every statistical inference rests on a chain of reasoning that runs from raw observations backward to population parameters. The validity of that chain depends almost entirely on decisions made before a single measurement is taken: which units to observe, when to observe them, how many, and under what conditions. Data collection design is the discipline that makes those decisions principled rather than arbitrary. It transforms the vague question "how do I gather evidence?" into a precise engineering specification — a sampling frame, an allocation rule, a randomization protocol, and a stopping criterion — each chosen to minimize estimator variance subject to a cost constraint or to maximize statistical power subject to an error-rate constraint.

Formally, let $\theta \in \Theta$ be the parameter of interest and let $\hat{\theta}_n$ be an estimator built from $n$ observations drawn according to design $\mathcal{D}$. The mean-squared error decomposes as

$$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_n;\,\mathcal{D}) = \mathrm{Bias}^2(\hat{\theta}_n;\,\mathcal{D}) + \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n;\,\mathcal{D}).$$

Data collection design operates on both terms simultaneously. A poorly chosen $\mathcal{D}$ inflates bias through systematic exclusion of subpopulations, while an under-powered $\mathcal{D}$ leaves variance unacceptably large. Optimal design theory seeks the $\mathcal{D}^$ that achieves a target $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_n;\,\mathcal{D}^) \leq \epsilon$ at minimum cost $c(n,\mathcal{D})$.

Worked example. Suppose a university wishes to estimate the mean weekly study time $\mu$ across $N = 4{,}000$ enrolled students, stratified into four colleges of unequal size $N_h$ and unequal within-stratum standard deviation $\sigma_h$. Under proportional allocation, $n_h \propto N_h$; under optimal (Neyman) allocation,

$$n_h = n \cdot \frac{N_h \sigma_h}{\sum_{k} N_k \sigma_k}.$$

If college engineering has $N_1 = 800$ and $\sigma_1 = 6.2$ hours while college arts has $N_2 = 1{,}200$ and $\sigma_2 = 2.1$ hours, Neyman allocation directs proportionally more interviews toward engineering, reducing $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{y}_{st})$ by up to 30 % relative to proportional allocation at the same total $n$.

Key theorem (Cramér–Rao lower bound). For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of a scalar parameter $\theta$ in a regular model,

$$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{I_n(\theta)},$$

where $I_n(\theta) = n\, I_1(\theta)$ is the Fisher information from $n$ independent observations. The bound makes design actionable: increasing $n$, improving measurement precision (reducing $\sigma$), or stratifying on a covariate correlated with the outcome all increase $I_n(\theta)$ and thereby tighten the bound. No inference procedure can compensate downstream for information that was never collected.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, simplify

N1, N2, s1, s2, n = symbols('N1 N2 s1 s2 n', positive=True)

# Neyman allocation fractions
w1 = (N1 * s1) / (N1 * s1 + N2 * s2)
w2 = (N2 * s2) / (N1 * s1 + N2 * s2)

# Variance of stratified estimator (two strata, large N)
var_st = (w1 * s1**2 / (n * w1) + w2 * s2**2 / (n * w2))
var_st_simplified = simplify(var_st)

print("Var(ȳ_st) under Neyman =", var_st_simplified)

# Numerical check: engineering vs arts example
vals = {N1: 800, N2: 1200, s1: 6.2, s2: 2.1, n: 200}
print("Numerical variance:", float(var_st_simplified.subs(vals)))

The session above lets you vary stratum sizes and spread, observe the allocation shift, and confirm that Neyman allocation strictly dominates proportional allocation whenever $\sigma_h$ differ across strata — a result provable in two lines of algebra yet invisible until the design is written down explicitly.

An invitation. Data collection design is the skeleton on which every subsequent chapter of applied statistics hangs. To see it at full resolution, turn to the chapter on randomized controlled trials in clinical research: there you will watch these allocation and power calculations save lives by determining, before a single patient is enrolled, exactly how large a trial must be to detect a clinically meaningful treatment effect with prescribed confidence — and why getting that number wrong in either direction carries real ethical weight.
