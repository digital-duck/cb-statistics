Frequency Table

Definition. Given a dataset $X = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$ drawn from a finite sample space $\Omega$, a frequency table is a mapping $f : \Omega \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ where $f(v)$ counts the number of observations equal to $v$. The relative frequency of $v$ is defined as

$$\hat{p}(v) = \frac{f(v)}{n}, \qquad \sum_{v \in \Omega} \hat{p}(v) = 1.$$

The relative frequency $\hat{p}(v)$ serves as the empirical counterpart to the theoretical probability $P(X = v)$: it reports the proportion of the sample that takes value $v$, and the normalization condition confirms that these proportions account for every observation exactly once.

Worked Example. Suppose a class of $n = 20$ students scores the following grades on a quiz (out of 5):

$$3, 4, 5, 3, 2, 4, 4, 5, 3, 3, 1, 4, 5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 3, 2, 4.$$

Tallying each distinct value:

| Score $v$ | Frequency $f(v)$ | Relative Frequency $\hat{p}(v)$ | |:---------:|:----------------:|:--------------------------------:| | 1 | 1 | 0.05 | | 2 | 3 | 0.15 | | 3 | 6 | 0.30 | | 4 | 6 | 0.30 | | 5 | 4 | 0.20 |

The scores 3 and 4 are tied for the highest frequency, and the sum of all relative frequencies equals $1.00$, confirming the normalization condition.

Key Theorem (Convergence of Relative Frequencies). Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent and identically distributed random variables with true probability mass function $p(v) = P(X = v)$. Then by the Strong Law of Large Numbers,

$$\hat{p}_n(v) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}[X_i = v] \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} p(v) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

This guarantees that with probability one, relative frequencies converge pointwise to their population counterparts as sample size grows. The frequency table is therefore a reliable empirical approximation of the true distribution whenever $n$ is large.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import *
from collections import Counter

scores = [3,4,5,3,2,4,4,5,3,3,1,4,5,2,3,4,5,3,2,4]
n = len(scores)
counts = Counter(scores)

for v in sorted(counts):
    rel = Rational(counts[v], n)
    print(f"Score {v}: f={counts[v]}, p̂={rel} = {float(rel):.2f}")

Running this cell reproduces the table above with exact rational arithmetic, illustrating that $\sum_v \hat{p}(v) = 1$ holds precisely rather than being subject to floating-point rounding error.
