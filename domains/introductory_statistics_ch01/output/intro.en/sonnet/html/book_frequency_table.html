Concept Book: Frequency Table

Introductory Statistics Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A frequency table is the first act of reduction that transforms raw observation into structured knowledge. After building the conceptual scaffolding of sets, mappings, counting measures, and probability mass functions, the frequency table stands as the natural endpoint because it is precisely the finite realization of all those abstractions: it assigns to each observed value $x_i$ in a sample $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$ an exact count $f_i = |\{j : x_j = x_i\}|$, together with a relative frequency $\hat{p}_i = f_i / n$ that estimates the true probability $p_i = P(X = x_i)$.

Definition. Let $\Omega = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}$ be the support of a discrete random variable $X$. A frequency table on a sample of size $n$ is the function $F : \Omega \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ defined by $F(v_j) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}[x_i = v_j]$, subject to the constraint $\sum_{j=1}^{k} F(v_j) = n$.

Worked Example. Suppose a class of $n = 20$ students reports study hours per day, yielding values in $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ with counts $F = (3, 6, 7, 3, 1)$. The relative frequencies are $\hat{p} = (0.15, 0.30, 0.35, 0.15, 0.05)$, and one verifies $\sum \hat{p}_j = 1$. The sample mean is estimated as $\bar{x} = \sum_j v_j \hat{p}_j = 1(0.15) + 2(0.30) + 3(0.35) + 4(0.15) + 5(0.05) = 2.80$ hours.

Key Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli, informal). As $n \to \infty$, the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_j$ converges uniformly to the true distribution $p_j$. Formally, $\sup_{v \in \Omega} |\hat{F}_n(v) - F(v)| \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0$, guaranteeing that the frequency table is a consistent estimator of the underlying probability law.

# Lab cell
from sympy import Rational, symbols
from collections import Counter

data = [1,2,2,3,3,3,2,4,3,2,1,3,5,2,3,4,1,3,2,4]
n = len(data)
counts = Counter(data)
table = {v: (counts[v], Rational(counts[v], n)) for v in sorted(counts)}
for v, (f, p) in table.items():
    print(f"value={v}  count={f}  rel_freq={p}")

Connection to Data Collection Design. The frequency table is not merely a summary device — it is the primary instrument that guides how data should be collected in the first place. A designer who anticipates the support $\Omega$ and the minimum detectable effect size can use the table's precision requirement, $\text{Var}(\hat{p}_j) = p_j(1-p_j)/n$, to compute the sample size $n$ needed before a single observation is recorded. Every choice of stratum, survey item, or experimental cell is answerable to the question: will the resulting frequency table have enough resolution to distinguish the effects we care about? The frequency table thus closes the loop between statistical inference and experimental design — it is simultaneously the output of a study and the specification for the next one.

You now hold every tool needed to move from concept to practice. The most rewarding next step is data collection design: given a research question, derive the required sample size, choose a sampling scheme whose strata align with your support $\Omega$, and verify post-hoc that the observed frequency table achieves the precision your analysis demands.
