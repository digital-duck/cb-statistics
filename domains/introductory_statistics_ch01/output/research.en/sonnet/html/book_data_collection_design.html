Concept Book: Data Collection Design

Introductory Statistics Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the concept achieves. Every preceding chapter in this text has sharpened one instrument in the analyst's kit — probability spaces, estimators, hypothesis tests, regression models. Data collection design is the decision that precedes all of them: it specifies the structure of the sample before a single observation is recorded. A well-designed collection protocol guarantees that the data carry, by construction, enough information to answer the scientific question at hand — and no amount of post-hoc statistical ingenuity can compensate for a design that discards that information at the source.

Formally, let $\mathcal{D} = (S, \mathcal{F}, P_\theta)_{\theta \in \Theta}$ denote a statistical experiment, where $S$ is the sample space, $\mathcal{F}$ a $\sigma$-algebra of events, and $\{P_\theta\}$ a parametric family. A data collection design is a measurable map

$$\delta : \Theta \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathbb{R}^n$$

where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of controllable covariates (treatment levels, sampling strata, measurement times), and $n$ is the number of planned observations. The design optimises an objective functional — commonly the Fisher information $\mathcal{I}_\delta(\theta)$ or the expected posterior variance — subject to budget and feasibility constraints.

Worked example. Suppose a researcher wishes to estimate the slope $\beta_1$ in the simple linear model $Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$, with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ and $x_i \in [0, 1]$. The variance of the ordinary-least-squares estimator satisfies

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2}.$$

Minimising this variance over all placements of $n$ points in $[0,1]$ yields the D-optimal design: place exactly $n/2$ observations at $x = 0$ and $n/2$ at $x = 1$. The proof follows directly from maximising the denominator, which is maximised when half the mass sits at each extreme of the design interval.

Key theorem (Kiefer–Wolfowitz, 1960). A design $\delta^$ is D-optimal for estimating $\theta$ in a linear model if and only if the maximum of the standardised variance function $d(x, \delta^) = \mathbf{f}(x)^\top \mathcal{I}(\delta^)^{-1} \mathbf{f}(x)$ over the design region equals the number of model parameters $p$, i.e., $\max_x\, d(x, \delta^) = p$. This equivalence theorem transforms an optimisation over infinite-dimensional spaces into a finite pointwise check.

Lab cell.

import numpy as np
from sympy import *

x, n = symbols('x n', positive=True)
# Variance of slope estimator for two-point design on [0,1]
# n/2 points at 0, n/2 at 1 => sum of (xi - xbar)^2
var_num = symbols('sigma2', positive=True)
SS = Rational(1,2)*n*(0 - Rational(1,2))**2 + Rational(1,2)*n*(1 - Rational(1,2))**2
var_beta1 = var_num / SS
print("Var(beta_1) =", simplify(var_beta1))   # sigma2 / (n/2)

Invitation. The D-optimality criterion demonstrated here extends far beyond simple regression: it governs the design of clinical trials, environmental sampling networks, and machine-learning active-learning loops. Choose one of these arenas and ask: what does the constraint set $\mathcal{C}$ look like in practice, and which nuisance parameters threaten the optimality guarantee? The chapters that follow take up exactly this challenge.
