Concept Book: Frequency Table

Introductory Statistics Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A frequency table is the first act of making sense of raw data. After collecting observations — counts of events, categorical responses, or discretized measurements — the analyst faces an unordered multiset with no visible structure. The frequency table imposes that structure by mapping each distinct value $x_i$ in the sample space $\Omega$ to its absolute count $f_i$ and relative frequency $\hat{p}_i = f_i / n$, where $n = \sum_i f_i$ is the total number of observations. The resulting object is a discrete probability distribution estimated from evidence, and it is the natural endpoint of an introductory data-collection workflow precisely because it transforms a list into a distribution: every subsequent tool — hypothesis test, confidence interval, regression — operates on distributional summaries, not raw lists.

To make this concrete, suppose a survey of $n = 120$ students records preferred study environment: Library (48), Home (36), Café (24), Lab (12). The absolute frequencies sum to 120, and the relative frequencies are $\hat{p}_{\text{lib}} = 0.40$, $\hat{p}_{\text{home}} = 0.30$, $\hat{p}_{\text{café}} = 0.20$, $\hat{p}_{\text{lab}} = 0.10$. Already one can state: the modal category is Library, and the top two categories account for $70\%$ of responses — conclusions invisible in the raw list.

Theorem (Convergence of empirical frequencies). Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. draws from a distribution $P$ over a finite alphabet $\Omega$. For each $x \in \Omega$, define $\hat{p}_n(x) = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[X_i = x]$. Then by the Strong Law of Large Numbers, $\hat{p}_n(x) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} P(x)$ as $n \to \infty$. Consequently the frequency table is a consistent estimator of the true underlying distribution.

This convergence result is what connects the frequency table to data-collection design: the designer's central task is choosing $n$ large enough that $\hat{p}_n$ is close to $P$ with high probability, and stratifying or weighting the sample so that every subgroup of interest appears in the table with adequate count. Poorly designed collection — undercoverage, convenience sampling, insufficient replication — produces a table whose cells are statistically unreliable, rendering every downstream analysis meaningless. The frequency table, in other words, is not only the output of data collection; it is the criterion against which the design is evaluated.

import sympy as sp
from sympy.stats import DiscreteUniform, density, Symbol

# Symbolic relative-frequency check
categories = ["Library", "Home", "Cafe", "Lab"]
counts     = [48, 36, 24, 12]
n          = sum(counts)
rel_freq   = {cat: sp.Rational(c, n) for cat, c in zip(categories, counts)}
print("Relative frequencies:", rel_freq)
print("Sum to 1?", sum(rel_freq.values()) == 1)

With the frequency table fully in hand, you are ready to ask sharper questions: How large must $n$ be to estimate each $\hat{p}_i$ within $\pm 0.03$ with $95\%$ confidence?
