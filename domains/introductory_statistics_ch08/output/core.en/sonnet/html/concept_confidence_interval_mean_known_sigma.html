Confidence Interval Mean Known Sigma

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed random variables with unknown mean $\mu$ and known population standard deviation $\sigma$. A $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval for $\mu$ is an interval $[L, U]$, constructed from the sample, such that

$$P(L \leq \mu \leq U) = 1 - \alpha.$$

Because the population is approximately normal (by the Central Limit Theorem for large $n$), the sample mean $\bar{X}$ satisfies

$$Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1).$$

Letting $z_{\alpha/2}$ denote the upper $\alpha/2$ critical value of the standard normal, we obtain the symmetric interval

$$\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

The quantity $z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is called the margin of error; it shrinks as $n$ increases and grows as $\sigma$ or $z_{\alpha/2}$ increases.

Worked Example. A biologist measures the resting heart rates (bpm) of $n = 36$ volunteers. The sample mean is $\bar{x} = 72.4$ bpm, and from prior large-scale studies the population standard deviation is known to be $\sigma = 9$ bpm. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true mean heart rate $\mu$.

For $1-\alpha = 0.95$ we have $\alpha/2 = 0.025$, giving $z_{0.025} = 1.96$. The margin of error is

$$E = 1.96 \cdot \frac{9}{\sqrt{36}} = 1.96 \cdot 1.5 = 2.94.$$

The 95% confidence interval is therefore

$$[72.4 - 2.94,\; 72.4 + 2.94] = [69.46,\; 75.34] \text{ bpm.}$$

Interpretation: If we were to repeat this sampling procedure many times, approximately 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population mean. The interval does not assert that $\mu$ has a 95% probability of lying in this particular range; $\mu$ is a fixed (unknown) constant.

Key Theorem (Coverage Guarantee). Under the assumptions above, $P\!\left(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \leq \mu \leq \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1 - \alpha$ exactly when the population is normal, and approximately for large $n$ by the Central Limit Theorem.
