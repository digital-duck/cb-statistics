Confidence Interval for the Mean with Unknown $\sigma$

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be an independent and identically distributed random sample from a normal population with unknown mean $\mu$ and unknown variance $\sigma^2$. In the previous section, $\sigma$ was assumed known, enabling a standard normal pivot. When $\sigma$ is unknown, we replace it with the sample standard deviation $S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}$, and this substitution changes the distribution of the pivotal quantity. The single new primitive this section introduces is Student's $t$-distribution: the quantity

$$T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S / \sqrt{n}}$$

follows a $t$-distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom. The $t_{n-1}$ distribution is bell-shaped and symmetric about zero, but has heavier tails than the standard normal, reflecting the additional uncertainty from estimating $\sigma$. A $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence interval for $\mu$ is therefore

$$\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\, n-1} \cdot \frac{S}{\sqrt{n}},$$

where $t_{\alpha/2,\, n-1}$ is the upper $\alpha/2$ critical value of the $t_{n-1}$ distribution.

Worked Example. A pharmacologist records the dissolution times (in minutes) of eight tablets: $\{14.2,\ 13.8,\ 15.1,\ 14.6,\ 13.9,\ 14.8,\ 15.3,\ 14.4\}$. We compute $\bar{x} = 14.5125$ and $s = 0.5357$. For a 95% interval with $n - 1 = 7$ degrees of freedom, the critical value is $t_{0.025,\,7} = 2.365$. The margin of error is

$$E = 2.365 \times \frac{0.5357}{\sqrt{8}} = 2.365 \times 0.1894 \approx 0.448.$$

The 95% confidence interval is $(14.064,\ 14.961)$ minutes. Were the sampling procedure repeated many times, approximately 95% of such intervals would contain the true mean $\mu$.

Key Theorem (Validity of the $t$-pivot). If $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $\bar{X}$ and $S^2$ are independent and the ratio $T = (\bar{X} - \mu)/(S/\sqrt{n})$ follows exactly a $t_{n-1}$ distribution — the interval above is therefore exact, not approximate, under normality. For non-normal populations, the Central Limit Theorem ensures approximate validity when $n \geq 30$, though heavier-tailed populations may require larger samples.

Lab Cell (SymPy/SciPy).

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

data = [14.2, 13.8, 15.1, 14.6, 13.9, 14.8, 15.3, 14.4]
n, xbar, s = len(data), np.mean(data), np.std(data, ddof=1)
alpha = 0.05
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha / 2, df=n - 1)
margin = t_crit * s / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"95% CI: ({xbar - margin:.3f}, {xbar + margin:.3f})")
