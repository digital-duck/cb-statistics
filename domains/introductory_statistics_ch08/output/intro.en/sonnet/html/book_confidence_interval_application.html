Concept Book: Confidence Interval Application

Introductory Statistics Ch08 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A confidence interval distills the entire arc of inferential statistics into a single, actionable statement: given a sample, how precisely can we bound the unknown population parameter? Every concept in this book—random variables, sampling distributions, the Central Limit Theorem, hypothesis testing—converges on this one construction. The confidence interval for a population mean is defined as follows.

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be an independent and identically distributed sample with unknown mean $\mu$ and known (or estimated) standard deviation $\sigma$. A $(1-\alpha)$-level confidence interval for $\mu$ is the random interval

$$\left(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\quad \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right),$$

where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the upper $\alpha/2$ critical value of the standard normal distribution. The interval is said to capture $\mu$ with probability $1-\alpha$.

Worked Example. A quality-control engineer measures the fill volume (in mL) of $n = 36$ bottles, obtaining $\bar{x} = 500.4$ mL and $s = 1.8$ mL. Constructing a 95% interval ($z_{0.025} = 1.96$):

$$500.4 \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{1.8}{\sqrt{36}} = 500.4 \pm 0.588 \implies (499.81,\; 500.99).$$

Because the nominal fill of 500 mL lies inside this interval, the engineer has no statistical grounds to halt the line.

Key Theorem (Coverage Guarantee). If the sampling distribution of $\bar{X}$ is exactly (or asymptotically, by the CLT) normal, then the procedure above produces intervals that contain the true $\mu$ in exactly $(1-\alpha) \times 100\%$ of independent replications. Coverage is a frequency property of the procedure, not a probability statement about a single realized interval.

Lab Cell.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
sample = rng.normal(loc=500, scale=1.8, size=36)

mean = sample.mean()
se   = stats.sem(sample)
ci   = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(sample)-1, loc=mean, scale=se)

print(f"x̄ = {mean:.3f},  95% CI = ({ci[0]:.3f}, {ci[1]:.3f})")

The confidence interval is not merely a formula—it is the lens through which every quantitative field reads uncertainty. Pharmaceutical trials report it to regulators; economists embed it in policy forecasts; physicists quote it alongside every measured constant. Each domain asks the same question and receives the same answer in the same language. Choose one application that matters to you and carry this construction all the way through: the interval you compute will be the last word your data can honestly speak.
