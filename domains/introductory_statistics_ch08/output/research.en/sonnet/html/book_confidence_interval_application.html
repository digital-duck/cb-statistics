Concept Book: Confidence Interval Application

Introductory Statistics Ch08 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

A confidence interval transforms a single numerical estimate into a calibrated statement about uncertainty. Where a point estimate such as $\hat{\mu} = 4.73$ tells us only what the data suggest on average, the corresponding $95\%$ confidence interval

$$\left[\hat{\mu} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \hat{\mu} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right]$$

guarantees that, across many independent replications of the same experiment, exactly $(1-\alpha)\times 100\%$ of such intervals will contain the true population parameter $\mu$. The interval is not a probability statement about $\mu$ itself — $\mu$ is fixed — but about the stochastic procedure that produced the bounds. This distinction, subtle yet fundamental, is precisely what makes confidence intervals the natural endpoint of inferential reasoning: they honour both the information in the data and the irreducible randomness of the sampling process.

Worked example. Suppose a clinical trial measures the reduction in systolic blood pressure (mmHg) for $n = 36$ patients, yielding $\bar{x} = 8.4$ and sample standard deviation $s = 6.0$. With $\alpha = 0.05$ and $z_{0.025} = 1.96$, the margin of error is

$$E = 1.96 \cdot \frac{6.0}{\sqrt{36}} = 1.96 \cdot 1.0 = 1.96,$$

giving the interval $[6.44,\; 10.36]$. Because the entire interval lies above zero, we conclude with $95\%$ confidence that the treatment produces a clinically meaningful reduction — a conclusion a bare $p$-value cannot convey.

Theorem (Coverage guarantee). Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known. Then

$$P\!\left(\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \leq \mu \leq \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1 - \alpha.$$

Proof sketch. Standardise: $Z = \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}-\mu)/\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The event $|Z| \leq z_{\alpha/2}$ has probability $1-\alpha$ by construction of the critical value, and rearranging the inequality in $Z$ yields the stated interval for $\mu$. $\square$

import sympy as sp

x_bar, sigma, n, z = 8.4, 6.0, 36, 1.96
E = z * sigma / sp.sqrt(n)
lower, upper = x_bar - E, x_bar + E
print(f"95% CI: [{float(lower):.4f}, {float(upper):.4f}]")

The machinery developed here — sampling distributions, the Central Limit Theorem, and the algebra of standardisation — reaches its full power the moment it is applied to a real decision. Choose one application that matters to you: A/B testing in a product launch, estimating a population proportion in a public-health survey, or bounding the mean lifetime of a manufactured component. In each case, the confidence interval is not the end of the analysis; it is the lens through which data become actionable knowledge.
