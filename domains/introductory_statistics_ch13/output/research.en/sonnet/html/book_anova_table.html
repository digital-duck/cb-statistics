What the ANOVA Table Achieves

Every preceding chapter of this book has built one layer of the inferential scaffold: populations and sampling distributions, the geometry of sums of squares, the $F$-distribution, and the partition of variance. The ANOVA table is the structure that assembles those layers into a single, auditable object. Given a dataset partitioned into $k$ groups with a grand total of $N$ observations, the table records

$$SS_{\text{total}} = SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{within}},$$

together with the corresponding degrees of freedom $k-1$ and $N-k$, the mean squares $MS_{\text{between}} = SS_{\text{between}}/(k-1)$ and $MS_{\text{within}} = SS_{\text{within}}/(N-k)$, and the test statistic

$$F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}},$$

which follows an $F(k-1,\, N-k)$ distribution under the null hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_k$. The table is not merely a computational convenience; it is a proof-of-work receipt showing every quantity a skeptical reader needs to verify the analysis independently.

Worked Example

Suppose a nutritionist measures the daily protein intake (g) of students in three dining plans: Plan A ($n_1 = 10$), Plan B ($n_2 = 10$), Plan C ($n_3 = 10$). She computes $SS_{\text{between}} = 420$, $SS_{\text{within}} = 540$. The ANOVA table reads:

| Source | SS | df | MS | $F$ | |---|---|---|---|---| | Between | 420 | 2 | 210 | 11.67 | | Within | 540 | 27 | 20 | | | Total | 960 | 29 | | |

With $F_{0.05}(2, 27) \approx 3.35$, the observed $F = 11.67$ falls in the rejection region, providing strong evidence that at least one plan mean differs.

Key Theorem

Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions (independence, normality, equal variance), the ratio $MS_{\text{between}}/MS_{\text{within}}$ is an unbiased estimator of 1 when $H_0$ is true, and exceeds 1 in expectation proportional to the non-centrality parameter $\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i(\mu_i - \mu)^2/\sigma^2$ when $H_0$ is false.

Lab Cell

import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

data = {"A": [72,68,74,70,66], "B": [80,85,78,82,79], "C": [65,70,68,63,67]}
groups = [v for v in data.values()]
F, p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print(f"F = {F:.3f}, p = {p:.4f}")

The ANOVA table is the natural endpoint of this book because it converts the abstract algebra of variance decomposition into a decision rule with quantifiable error rates. Every application that requires comparing more than two means — clinical trials assessing drug dosages, agricultural experiments varying fertilizer treatments, A/B/n product tests with multiple interface variants — reduces, at its statistical core, to reading and interpreting this table. Mastering it means you hold a key that unlocks the entire landscape of designed experiments. The next chapter invites you to turn that key in one direction: factorial designs, where two or more factors interact, and the ANOVA table expands to reveal effects that no single-factor analysis could expose.
