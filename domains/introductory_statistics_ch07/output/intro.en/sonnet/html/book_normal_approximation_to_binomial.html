Concept Book: Normal Approximation To Binomial

Introductory Statistics Ch07 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The journey through this text has assembled, brick by brick, the machinery required to answer a deceptively simple question: when a coin is flipped one thousand times, how likely is it that heads appears between 480 and 520 times? Computing this exactly demands summing more than forty binomial coefficients, each involving factorials of three-digit numbers. The Normal Approximation to the Binomial collapses that computation to a single evaluation of the standard normal cumulative distribution function, and it does so with a precision that is provably controllable.

Definition. Let $X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)$. For large $n$, the standardized variable

$$Z = \frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}$$

converges in distribution to $Z_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Concretely, for any $a < b$,

$$P(a \leq X \leq b) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{b + \tfrac{1}{2} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{a - \tfrac{1}{2} - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right),$$

where the shifts of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ constitute the continuity correction, and $\Phi$ denotes the standard normal CDF. The Berry–Esseen theorem quantifies the error: the approximation deviates from the true probability by at most $C/\sqrt{n}$ uniformly over all intervals, with $C < 0.5$.

Worked Example. A fair coin ($p = 0.5$) is tossed $n = 1000$ times. What is the probability that the number of heads $X$ satisfies $480 \leq X \leq 520$?

The mean is $\mu = np = 500$ and the standard deviation is $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)} = \sqrt{250} \approx 15.81$. Applying the continuity correction:

$$P(480 \leq X \leq 520) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{520.5 - 500}{15.81}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{479.5 - 500}{15.81}\right) = \Phi(1.297) - \Phi(-1.297) \approx 0.9056 - 0.0944 = 0.811.$$

A direct binomial summation yields $0.8132$, confirming the approximation is accurate to three significant figures.

Theorem (Central Limit Theorem for Bernoulli Trials). If $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ are independent $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ random variables, then

$$\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

This is not merely an algebraic trick: it is the statement that the aggregate behavior of many independent binary events, regardless of the underlying probability $p$, is governed by a single universal curve. Every quality-control chart, every clinical trial power calculation, every polling margin of error rests on precisely this result.

from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Rational, N
from sympy.stats import Normal, P, Binomial
from sympy import oo

# Exact binomial probability
X = Binomial('X', 1000, Rational(1, 2))
exact = P((X >= 480) & (X <= 520))

# Normal approximation with continuity correction
mu, sigma = 500, sqrt(250)
Z = Normal('Z', 0, 1)
approx = P((Z >= (Rational(4795, 10) - mu) / sigma)
         & (Z <= (Rational(5205, 10) - mu) / sigma))

print(f"Exact:  {N(exact, 6)}")
print(f"Approx: {N(approx, 6)}")

The normal approximation to the binomial is the natural capstone of this text because it is where counting meets continuous analysis, where combinatorial exactness yields—gracefully—to analytical tractability. The same standardization step unlocks hypothesis testing, confidence intervals for proportions, and the design of randomized experiments. You now hold the key to all of them.
