What the Normal Approximation to the Binomial Achieves

The binomial distribution $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$ answers a precise question: what is the probability of exactly $k$ successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials? The exact answer,

$$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k},$$

is computationally expensive for large $n$ and analytically opaque — it reveals almost nothing about the shape or spread of the distribution. The normal approximation replaces this expression with a tractable continuous model. By the Central Limit Theorem, when $np \geq 5$ and $n(1-p) \geq 5$,

$$\frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \xrightarrow{d} Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1),$$

so that cumulative probabilities reduce to evaluating the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. This is not merely a computational shortcut; it is a conceptual unification — it reveals that the binomial is one member of a vast family of distributions that all converge to the same bell-shaped limit.

Why It Is the Natural Endpoint

Every concept in this book has built toward this result. Counting principles supply the binomial coefficient. Probability axioms justify independence. Expectation and variance provide the centering and scaling constants $\mu = np$ and $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)$. The normal distribution furnishes the limiting shape. The approximation is the convergence point where discrete probability meets continuous analysis — the first genuinely powerful bridge between combinatorics and calculus-based statistics.

A Worked Example

Suppose a manufacturing process produces defective items with probability $p = 0.04$. A quality inspector examines $n = 200$ items. Let $X$ count the defectives. The exact probability $P(X \leq 12)$ requires summing thirteen binomial terms. Using the normal approximation with continuity correction:

$$\mu = 8, \quad \sigma = \sqrt{200 \cdot 0.04 \cdot 0.96} = \sqrt{7.68} \approx 2.77,$$ $$P(X \leq 12) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{12.5 - 8}{2.77}\right) = \Phi(1.624) \approx 0.9478.$$

The exact binomial value is $0.9493$ — an error under $0.2\%$, achieved with a single table lookup.

Key Theorem

Theorem (De Moivre–Laplace). Let $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$ with $0 < p < 1$. For any fixed $a < b$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P\!\left(a \leq \frac{X - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \leq b\right) = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a).$$

The convergence is uniform in $a$ and $b$, and the error in the cumulative approximation is $O(n^{-1/2})$.

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, binomial, Rational, N
from sympy.stats import Binomial, Normal, P, Le

n_val, p_val = 200, Rational(4, 100)
X = Binomial('X', n_val, p_val)
Z = Normal('Z', 0, 1)

mu = n_val * p_val
sigma = (n_val * p_val * (1 - p_val))**Rational(1, 2)

exact   = P(Le(X, 12))
approx  = P(Le(Z, (Rational(25, 2) - mu) / sigma))  # continuity correction

print("Exact  :", N(exact,  6))
print("Approx :", N(approx, 6))

An Invitation

The normal approximation is not the end of the story — it is the beginning of applications. In quality control, it underlies acceptance-sampling plans that govern every production line. In polling and survey design, it determines the sample sizes that make margin-of-error guarantees possible. In genetics, it models allele frequencies across large populations. In finance, it approximates the distribution of portfolio returns built from many small, nearly independent shocks.

Choose any one of these domains and you will find that the approximation you have just mastered is the single most-used tool in the practitioner's kit.
