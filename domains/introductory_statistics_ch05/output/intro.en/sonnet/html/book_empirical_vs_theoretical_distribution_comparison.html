What the Comparison Achieves

Throughout this chapter we have built the machinery to describe random phenomena both from data and from first principles. The concept of empirical versus theoretical distribution comparison is the moment those two threads converge: it is the formal act of asking whether a theoretical model—chosen on mechanistic or simplicity grounds—is consistent with evidence collected from the real world.

Concretely, given $n$ independent observations $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ drawn from an unknown population, we form the empirical cumulative distribution function

$$\hat{F}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}[x_i \le x],$$

and compare it against a proposed theoretical CDF $F_0(x)$. The supremum of pointwise discrepancies,

$$D_n = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \left| \hat{F}_n(x) - F_0(x) \right|,$$

is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic. Under the null hypothesis that the data truly come from $F_0$, the distribution of $\sqrt{n}\, D_n$ converges to the Kolmogorov distribution as $n \to \infty$—a distribution whose quantiles are tabulated and whose tail probabilities give us an actionable $p$-value.

Worked Example

Suppose a physicist records $n = 200$ inter-arrival times (in milliseconds) between cosmic-ray events and conjectures an exponential model with rate $\hat{\lambda} = \bar{x}^{-1}$. After computing $\hat{F}_{200}$ from the sorted data and evaluating $F_0(x) = 1 - e^{-\hat{\lambda} x}$ at each point, the analyst finds $D_{200} = 0.048$. The critical value at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ for $n = 200$ is approximately $0.096$. Since $D_{200} < 0.096$, the exponential hypothesis is not rejected—the model passes its first empirical test.

Key Theorem

Glivenko–Cantelli. For any continuous $F_0$, $D_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Consequently, $\hat{F}_n$ is a uniformly consistent estimator of the true CDF regardless of the shape of the population, which guarantees that the comparison procedure gains power with sample size.

Lab Cell (SymPy / SciPy)

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
data = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=200)

lam_hat = 1.0 / data.mean()
stat, p_value = stats.kstest(data, "expon", args=(0, 1 / lam_hat))
print(f"KS statistic = {stat:.4f},  p-value = {p_value:.4f}")

Running this cell makes the abstract comparison concrete: the printed $p$-value is the probability of seeing a discrepancy at least as large as the observed $D_n$ if the exponential model were true.

Invitation

This comparison is not a terminus but a gateway. In reliability engineering it distinguishes wear-out failures (Weibull tails) from memoryless ones (exponential), directly informing maintenance schedules. In finance it tests whether log-returns follow a normal or a heavy-tailed stable distribution, with profound implications for risk capital. In biology it validates whether neuron spike-trains obey a Poisson process or exhibit refractory-period clustering. In each domain, the same two-line statistical test carries discipline-specific weight.

Choose the domain that speaks most directly to your work, and carry the comparison forward: fit the theoretical parameters by maximum likelihood, interrogate the residuals with a Q–Q plot, and ask what scientific conclusion the accepted or rejected model entitles you to draw. That translation from statistical outcome to scientific claim is where the deepest learning begins.
