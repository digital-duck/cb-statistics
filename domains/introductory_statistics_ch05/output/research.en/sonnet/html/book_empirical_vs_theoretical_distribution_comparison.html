Concept Book: Empirical Vs Theoretical Distribution Comparison

Introductory Statistics Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Definition. Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed random variables drawn from an unknown population. The empirical distribution function is

$$F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[X_i \leq x],$$

a step function that places mass $1/n$ at each observed value. A theoretical distribution function $F(x;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the cumulative distribution prescribed by a parametric model with parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Empirical-versus-theoretical distribution comparison is the systematic quantification of the discrepancy $\sup_x |F_n(x) - F(x;\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|$ and the statistical assessment of whether that discrepancy is consistent with sampling variability alone.

Worked Example. Suppose $n = 200$ waiting times (in minutes) are recorded at a hospital triage desk. We hypothesise that waiting time follows an exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$. The maximum-likelihood estimate is $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$. Plotting $F_n(x)$ against $F(x;\hat{\lambda}) = 1 - e^{-\hat{\lambda}x}$ reveals systematic curvature in the upper tail: the empirical function rises more slowly than the theoretical one, signalling heavier tails than the exponential model admits. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic of $D_n = 0.087$ with $p = 0.03$ confirms that the gap is unlikely under the null hypothesis. The analyst then considers a Weibull or log-normal alternative, re-fits, and repeats the comparison — each iteration guided by what the discrepancy plot reveals.

Key Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli). For any fixed distribution $F$,

$$\sup_x |F_n(x) - F(x)| \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

This uniform convergence — stronger than pointwise — guarantees that $F_n$ eventually resolves every feature of $F$ simultaneously, making the empirical function a faithful, model-free witness against which any parametric claim can be adjudicated.

Lab Cell (SymPy/SciPy).

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
data = rng.exponential(scale=5, size=200)     # true mean = 5
lam_hat = 1 / data.mean()

xs = np.linspace(0, data.max(), 500)
F_emp = np.array([(data <= x).mean() for x in xs])
F_theo = 1 - np.exp(-lam_hat * xs)

plt.plot(xs, F_emp,  label=r"$F_n(x)$")
plt.plot(xs, F_theo, label=r"$F(x;\hat\lambda)$", linestyle="--")
plt.legend(); plt.xlabel("x"); plt.ylabel("CDF"); plt.show()

stat, p = stats.kstest(data, "expon", args=(0, 1/lam_hat))
print(f"KS statistic = {stat:.4f},  p-value = {p:.4f}")

This comparison is the natural endpoint of a first course in mathematical statistics because it fuses every preceding idea into a single decision: probability axioms justify $F$; limit theorems explain why $F_n \to F$; estimation theory produces $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$; and hypothesis testing converts the visual gap into a defensible conclusion. Whether your data are survival times in medicine, photon-count distributions in astrophysics, or score distributions in educational assessment, the question is always the same — does my model account for what the data actually show? — and empirical-versus-theoretical comparison is the language in which that question is answered with precision.
