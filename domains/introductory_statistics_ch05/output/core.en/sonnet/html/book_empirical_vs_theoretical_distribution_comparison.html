Concept Book: Empirical Vs Theoretical Distribution Comparison

Introductory Statistics Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Definition. The empirical distribution of a dataset $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$ is the probability measure that places mass $1/n$ at each observed value, yielding the empirical cumulative distribution function

$$\hat{F}_n(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[x_i \leq t].$$

A theoretical distribution $F_\theta$ is a parametric model indexed by $\theta \in \Theta$. Empirical-versus-theoretical distribution comparison is the systematic programme of measuring how well $F_\theta$ accounts for $\hat{F}_n$, using metrics that are both statistically principled and computationally tractable.

Every preceding concept in this textbook — random variables, expectation, variance, the Central Limit Theorem, parameter estimation, and hypothesis testing — has been preparation for this single act of confrontation: placing the model beside the data and asking, formally, whether they are consistent.

Worked example. Suppose $n = 200$ waiting times (in minutes) are recorded at a service desk, and a memoryless model suggests $X \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ with $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{x}$. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic

$$D_n = \sup_{t \geq 0}\bigl|\hat{F}_n(t) - F_{\hat{\lambda}}(t)\bigr|$$

measures the largest vertical gap between the two CDFs. For $n = 200$ at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, the critical value is approximately $0.096$. Computing $D_n$ numerically, if $D_n = 0.042 < 0.096$, we fail to reject the exponential model; if $D_n = 0.13 > 0.096$, the data expose a heavier tail than the model predicts, directing attention toward a gamma or log-normal alternative.

Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli). For any continuous distribution $F$, $\sup_t |\hat{F}_n(t) - F(t)| \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0$ as $n \to \infty$. This guarantee means that with sufficient data the empirical distribution will always reveal the truth, regardless of the hypothesised model — making the comparison methodology universally reliable in the large-sample regime.

Lab cell.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import sympy as sp

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
data = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=200)
lam_hat = 1 / data.mean()

stat, p_value = stats.kstest(data, 'expon', args=(0, 1/lam_hat))
print(f"KS statistic: {stat:.4f},  p-value: {p_value:.4f}")

# Symbolic CDF for inspection
t = sp.Symbol('t', positive=True)
F_theoretical = 1 - sp.exp(-lam_hat * t)
sp.pprint(F_theoretical)

The comparison framework generalises immediately: swap the exponential for a normal, Poisson, or power-law model, and the same KS or $\chi^2$ machinery applies unchanged. This universality is the deepest payoff. You now possess a single coherent lens through which measurement data, simulation output, financial returns, biological counts, and information-theoretic models can all be scrutinised against their theoretical idealisations.

To see this power fully deployed, turn to the next chapter, where we apply it to the analysis of real network traffic data — fitting, rejecting, and ultimately selecting a heavy-tailed model whose implications reshape how engineers provision bandwidth.
